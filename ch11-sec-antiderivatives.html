    Section 11.1 Antiderivatives

    Subsection 11.1.1 Definition

    Recall that we say \(F\) is an antiderivative of \(f\) if \(F' = f\text{.}\) Here are some examples.
    🔗
    Notice that you go up (\(\uparrow\)) from the bottom row

    Aside
    Undo a differentiation
    🔗
    to the top by carrying out a differentiation. To go down (\(\downarrow\)) you must “undo” that differentiation. The process of reversing, or undoing, a differentiation has come to be called antidifferentiation. You should differentiate each function on the bottom row to check that it is an antiderivative of the function above it.
    🔗
    While a function can have only one derivative,

    Aside
    A function has many antiderivatives
    🔗
    it has many antiderivatives. For example, \(1-\cos{u}\) and \(99 - \cos{u}\) are also antiderivatives of the function \(\sin{u}\) because
    \begin{equation*} (1-\cos{u})' = \sin{u} = (99 - \cos{u})'. \end{equation*}
    In fact, every function \(C - \cos{u}\) is an antiderivative of \(\sin{u}\text{,}\) for any constant \(C\) whatsoever. This observation is true in general. That is, if \(F\) is an antiderivative of a function \(f\text{,}\) then so is \(F + C\text{,}\) for any constant \(C\text{.}\) This follows from the addition rule for derivatives:
    \begin{equation*} (F + C)' = F' + C' = F' + 0 = f. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    It is tempting to claim

    Aside
    A caution
    🔗
    the converse—that every antiderivative of \(f\) is equal to \(F + C\text{,}\) for some appropriately chosen value of \(C\text{.}\) In fact, you will often see this statement written. The statement is true, though, only for continuous functions. If the function \(f\) has breaks in its domain, then there will be more antiderivatives than those of the form \(F+C\) for a single constant \(C\)—over each piece of the domain of \(f\text{,}\) \(F\) can be modified by a different constant and still yield an antiderivative for \(f\text{.}\) Exercises 18, 19, and 20 at the end of this section explore this for a couple of cases. If \(f\) is continuous, though, \(F + C\) will cover all the possibilities, and

    Aside
    What the ‘\(+\ C\)’ term really means
    🔗
    we sometimes say that \(F + C\) is the antiderivative of \(f\text{.}\) For the sake of keeping a compact notation, we will even write this when the domain of \(f\) consists of more than one interval. You should understand, though, that in such cases, over each piece \(F\) can be modified by a different constant
    🔗
    For future reference we collect a list

    Aside
    Antiderivatives of basic functions
    🔗
    of basic functions whose antiderivatives we already know. Remember that each antiderivative in the table can have an arbitrary constant added to it.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \mbox{function} & \qquad & \mbox{antiderivative} \\[2pt] \hline \\ [-9pt] \begin{array}{l} x^p \\ [12pt] 1/x \\ [2pt] \sin{x} \\ [2pt] \cos{x} \\ [2pt] e^x \\ [6pt] b^x \end{array} & & \begin{array}{l} \hphantom{-}\dfrac{x^{p+1}}{p+1}, \quad p \neq -1 \\[10pt] \hphantom{-}\ln{x}\\ [2pt] -\cos{x}\\ [2pt] \hphantom{-}\sin{x}\\ [2pt] \hphantom{-}e^x \\[2pt] \hphantom{-}\dfrac{b^x}{\ln{b}} \end{array} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    All of these antiderivatives are easily verified and could have been derived with at most a little trial and error fiddling to get the right constant. You should notice one incongruity: the function \(1/x\) is defined for all \(x\neq 0\text{,}\) but its listed antiderivative, \(\ln{x}\text{,}\) is only defined for \(x>0\text{.}\) In exercise 18 (page ) you will see how to find antiderivatives for \(1/x\) over its entire domain.
    🔗
    Two of our basic functions—\(\ln{x}\) and \(\tan{x}\)—do not appear in the left column of the table. This happens because there is no simple multiple of a basic function whose derivative is equal to either \(\ln{x}\) or \(\tan{x}\text{.}\) It turns out that these functions do have antiderivatives, though, that can be expressed as more complicated combinations of basic functions. In fact, by differentiating \(x \ln{x} - x\) you should be able to verify that it is an antiderivative of \(\ln{x}\text{.}\) Likewise, \(- \ln(\cos{x})\) is an antiderivative of \(\tan{x}\text{.}\) It would take a long time to stumble on these antiderivatives by inspection or by trial and error. It is the purpose of later sections to develop techniques which will enable us to discover antiderivatives like these quickly and efficiently. In particular, the antiderivative of \(\ln{x}\) is derived in chapter 11.3 on page , while the antiderivative of \(\tan{x}\) is derived in chapter 11.5 on page .
    🔗
    There are a couple of other functions that don’t appear in the above table whose antiderivatives are needed frequently enough that they should become part of your repertoire of elementary functions that you recognize immediately:
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \mbox{function} & \qquad & \mbox{antiderivative} \\[2pt] \hline \\ [-9pt] \begin{array}{l} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}} \\ [10pt] \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\label{arcsin deriv} \end{array} & & \begin{array}{l} \hphantom{-} \arcsin{x} \\ [10pt] \hphantom{-} \arctan{x} \end{array} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    The antiderivatives are inverse trigonometric functions, which we’ve had no need for until now. We introduce them immediately below. They are examples of functions that occur more often for their antiderivative properties than for themselves. Note that the derivatives of the inverse trigonometric functions have no obvious reference to trigonometric relations. In fact, they often occur in settings where there are no triangles or periodic functions in sight. Let’s see how the derivatives of these inverse functions are derived.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.1.2 Inverse Functions

    We discussed inverse functions in chapter 4.4. Here’s a quick summary of the main points made there. Two functions \(f\) and \(g\) are inverses if
    \begin{align*} f(g(a)) \amp= a,\\ \text{and} \quad g(f(b)) \amp= b, \end{align*}
    for every \(a\) in the domain of \(g\) and every \(b\) in the domain of \(f\text{.}\) It follows that the range of \(f\) is the same as the domain of \(g\text{,}\) and vice versa. We write \(g = f^{-1}\) to indicate that \(g\) is the inverse of \(f\text{,}\) and \(f = g^{-1}\) to indicate that \(f\) is the inverse of \(g\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The graphs of \(y = f(x)\) and \(y = g(x)\) are mirror reflections about the line \(y=x\text{.}\) As the figure below shows, the mirror image of a point with coordinates \((a, b)\) is the point with coordinates \((b, a)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This connection between the graphs is a direct translation of the definitions into graphical language, since
    \begin{align*} text{$(a, b)$ is on the graph of $y=g(x)$} \amp\Longleftrightarrow g(a) = b \qquad \text{(definition of graph)}\\ \amp\Longleftrightarrow f(b)=a \qquad \text{(definition of inverse})\\ \amp\Longleftrightarrow \text{$(b, a)$ is on the graph of $y=f(x)$}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Because of the connection between the graphs, it follows immediately that if the graph of \(f\) is locally linear at the point \((b, a)\) with slope \(m\text{,}\) then the graph of \(g\) will be locally linear at the point \((a, b)\) with slope \(1/m\text{.}\) Algebraically, this is expressed as
    \begin{equation*} g'(a)=1/f'(b), \end{equation*}
    where \(a=f(b)\) and \(b=g(a)\text{.}\) We get same result by differentiating the expression \(f(g(x))=x\text{,}\) using the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} 1=x'=(f(g(x))'=f'(g(x))g'(x), \end{equation*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*} g'(x)=\dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))} \end{equation*}
    for any value of \(x\) for which \(g(x)\) is defined.
    🔗

    Inverse trigonometric functions.

    The arcsine functionIn the discussion in chapter 4 we saw that a function has an inverse only when it is one-to-one, so if we want an inverse, we often have to restrict the domain of a function to a region where it is one-to-one. This is certainly the case with the sine function, which takes the same value infinitely many times. The standard choice of domain on which the sine function is one-to-one is \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{.}\) Over this interval the sine function increases from \(-1\) to \(1\text{.}\) We can then define an inverse function, which we call the arcsine function, written \(\arcsin{x}\text{,}\) whose domain is the interval \([-1,1]\text{,}\) and whose range is \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{.}\) Since the sine function is strictly increasing on its domain, the arcsine function will be strictly increasing on its domain as well—do you see why this has to be?
    🔗
    Another notation for the arcsine function is \(\sin^{-1}{x}\text{;}\) this form commonly appears on one of the buttons on a calculator.
    🔗
    To find the derivative of the arcsine function, let \(f(x)=\sin{x}\text{,}\) and let \(g(x)=\arcsin{x}\text{.}\) Then by the remarks above, we have
    \begin{equation*} g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))} = \dfrac{1}{\cos{(g(x))}} = \dfrac{1}{\cos(\arcsin{x})}. \end{equation*}
    The function \(\cos(\arcsin{x})\) can be expressed in another form, which we will obtain two ways, one algebraic, the other geometric. Both perspectives are useful.
    🔗
    The algebraic approach.Recall that for any input \(t\text{,}\) \(\sin^2{t} + \cos^2{t}=1\text{.}\) This can be solved for \(\cos{t}\) as \(\cos{t}=\pm\sqrt{1-\sin^2{t}}\text{.}\) That is, the cosine of anything is the square root of 1 minus the square of the sine of that input, with a possible minus sign needed out front, depending on the context. Since the output of the arcsine function lies in the range \([-\pi/2, \pi/2]\text{,}\) and the cosine function is positive (or 0) for numbers in this interval, it follows that \(\cos(\arcsin{x})\geq 0\) for any value of \(x\) in the domain of the arcsine function. Therefore,
    \begin{equation*} \cos(\arcsin{x}) = \sqrt{1-\sin^2(\arcsin{x})} =\sqrt{1-(\sin(\arcsin{x}))^2}=\sqrt{1-x^2}, \end{equation*}
    since \(\sin(\arcsin{x})=x\) by definition of inverse functions. It follows that
    \begin{equation*} (\arcsin{x})'=\dfrac{1}{\cos{(\arcsin{x})}}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}, \end{equation*}
    as we indicated above on page .
    🔗
    The geometric approachIntroduce a new variable \(\theta = \arcsin{x}\text{,}\) so that \(x=\sin{\theta}\text{.}\) We can represent these relationships in the following picture:
    🔗
    Notice that we have labelled the side opposite the angle \(\theta\) as \(x\) and the hypotenuse as 1. This ensures that \(\sin\theta=x\text{.}\) By the Pythagorean theorem, the remaining side must then be \(\sqrt{1-x^2}\text{.}\) From this picture it is then obvious that \(\cos(\arcsin{x})=\cos\theta=\sqrt{1-x^2}/1=\sqrt{1-x^2},\) as before.
    🔗
    The arctangent functionTo get an inverse for the tangent function, we again need to limit its domain. Here the standard choice is to restrict it to the interval \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\) (not including the endpoints this time, since \(\tan(-\pi/2)\) and \(\tan(\pi/2)\) aren’t defined). Over this domain the tangent function increases from \(-\infty\) to \(+\infty\text{.}\) We can then define the inverse of the tangent, called the arctangent function, written \(\arctan{x}\text{,}\) whose domain is the interval \((-\infty, \infty)\text{,}\) and whose range is \((-\pi/2, \pi/2)\text{.}\) Again, both functions are increasing over their domains.
    🔗
    To find the derivative, we proceed as we did with \(\arcsin{x}\text{,}\) letting \(f(x)=\tan{x}\text{,}\) and \(g(x)=\arctan{x}\text{.}\) This time we get
    \begin{equation*} g'(x) = \dfrac{1}{f'(g(x))} = \dfrac{1}{\sec^2{(g(x))}} =\dfrac{1}{\sec^2(\arctan{x})}. \end{equation*}
    This expression also has a different form; we obtain it from another trigonometric identity, as we did before, deriving the desired result algebraically and geometrically.
    🔗
    AlgebraicWe start as before with the identity \(\sin^2{t} + \cos^2{t}=1\text{.}\) Dividing through by \(\cos^2{t}\text{,}\) we get the equivalent identity \(\tan^2{t}+1=\sec^2{t}\text{.}\) That is, the square of the secant of any input is just 1 plus the square of the tangent applied to the same input. In particular,
    \begin{equation*} \sec^2{(\arctan{x})} = \tan^2(\arctan{x}) + 1 =(\tan(\arctan{x}))^2 + 1 = x^2+1; \end{equation*}
    since \(\tan(\arctan{x}) = x\text{.}\) It follows that
    \begin{equation*} (\arctan{x})' = \dfrac{1}{\sec^2(\arctan{x})} = \dfrac{1}{1+x^2}, \end{equation*}
    as we indicated above on page .
    🔗
    GeometricLet \(\theta=\arctan{x}\text{,}\) so \(x=\tan\theta\text{.}\) Again we can draw and label a triangle reflecting these relationships:
    🔗
    Notice that this time we have labelled the side opposite the angle \(\theta\) as \(x\) and the adjacent side as 1 to ensure that \(\tan\theta=x\text{.}\) Again by the Pythagorean theorem, the hypotenuse must be \(1+x^2\text{.}\) From this picture it is then obvious that \(\sec(\arctan{x})=\sec\theta=(\sqrt{1+x^2})/1=\sqrt{1+x^2},\) so \(\sec^2(\arctan{x})=1+x^2\) again.

    Subsection 11.1.3 Notation

    According to the fundamental theorem of calculus (see chapter 6.4), every accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \end{equation*}
    is an antiderivative of the function \(f\text{,}\) no matter at what point \(t = a\) the accumulation begins—so long as the function is defined over the entire interval from \(t=a\) to \(t=x\text{.}\) (This is an important caution when dealing with functions like \(1/x\text{,}\) for instance, for which the integral from, say, \(-1\) to 1 makes no sense.) In other words, the expression
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^x f(t) \, dt \end{equation*}
    represents an antiderivative of \(f\text{.}\)

    Aside
    Write an antiderivative as an integral
    🔗
    The influence of the fundamental theorem is so pervasive that this expression—with the “limits of integration” \(a\) and \(x\) omitted—is used to denote an antiderivative:
    🔗
    With this new notation the antiderivatives we have listed so far can be written

    Aside
    The basic antiderivatives again
    🔗
    in the following form.
    \begin{align*} int x^p \, dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{p+1} \, x^{p+1} + C \qquad\mbox{ ( $p\neq -1$)}\\ \int \dfrac{1}{x} \, dx \amp= \ln{x} + C\\ \int \sin{x} \, dx \amp= - \cos{x} + C\\ \int \cos{x} \, dx \amp= \sin{x} + C\\ \int e^x \, dx \amp= e^x + C\\ \int b^x \, dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{\ln{b} \, b^x} + C\\ \int \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\, dx \amp= \arcsin{x} + C\\ \int \dfrac{1}{1+x^2} \, dx \amp= \arctan{x} + C \end{align*}
    🔗
    The integration sign \(\int\) now has two distinct meanings.

    Aside
    The definite integral is a number…
    🔗
    Originally, it was used to describe the number
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx, \end{equation*}
    which was always calculated as the limit of a sequence of Riemann sums. Because this integral has a definite numerical value, it is called the definite integral. In its new meaning, the integration sign is used to describe the antiderivative
    \begin{equation*} \int f(x) \, dx, \end{equation*}
    which is a function, not a number.

    Aside
    …while the indefinite integral is a function
    🔗
    To contrast the new use of \(\int\) with the old, and to remind us that the new expression is a variable quantity, it is called the indefinite integral. The function that appears in either a definite or an indefinite integral is called the integrand. The terms “antiderivative” and “indefinite integral” are completely synonymous. We will tend to use the former term in general discussions, using the latter term when focusing on the process of finding the antiderivative.
    🔗
    Because an indefinite integral represents an antiderivative, the process of finding an antiderivative is sometimes called integration. Thus the term integration, as well as the symbol for it, has two distinct meanings.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.1.4 Using Antiderivatives

    According to the fundamental theorem, we can use an indefinite integral to find the value of a definite integral—and this largely explains the importance of antiderivatives. In the language of indefinite integrals, the statement of the fundamental theorem in the box on page  takes the following form.
    🔗
    [[EX:1]]Find \(\dint_1^4 x^2 \, dx\text{.}\) We have that
    \begin{equation*} \int x^2 \, dx = \tfrac{1}{3} x^3 + C; \end{equation*}
    it follows that
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^4 x^2 \, dx = \tfrac{1}{3} 4^3 + C - \left(\tfrac{1}{3}1^3 +C)\right) = \dfrac{64}{3} + C - \dfrac{1}{3} - C = 21. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    [[EX:2]]Find \(\dint_0^{\pi/2} \cos{t} \, dt\text{.}\) This time the indefinite integral we need is
    \begin{equation*} \int \cos{t} \, dt = \sin{t} + C. \end{equation*}
    The value of the definite integral is therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos{t} \, dt = \sin{\pi/2} + C - \left( \sin{0} + C \right) = 1 + C - 0 - C = 1. \end{equation*}
    In each example the two appearances of \(C\) cancel each other.

    Aside
    The calculation doesn’t depend on \(C\text{,}\) so take \(C = 0\)
    🔗
    Thus \(C\) does not appear in the final result. This implies that it does not matter which value of \(C\) we choose to do the calculation. Usually, we just take \(C = 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Notation: Because expressions like \(F(b) - F(a)\) occur often when we are using indefinite integrals, we use the abbreviation
    \begin{equation*} F(b) - F(a) = \left. F(x) \rule{0pt}{14pt}\, \right|_a^b. \end{equation*}
    Thus,
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^4 x^2 \, dx = \left. \dfrac{x^3}{3} \, \right|_1^4 = \dfrac{64}{3} - \dfrac{1}{3} = 21. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    There are clear advantages to using antiderivatives to evaluate definite integrals: we get exact values and we avoid many lengthy calculations. The difficulty is that the method works only if we can find a formula for the antiderivative.
    🔗
    There are several reasons why we might not

    Aside
    We may not recognize the antiderivative…
    🔗
    find the formula we need. For instance, the antiderivative we want may be a function we have never seen before. The function \(\arctan{x}\) is an example.
    🔗
    Even if we have a broad acquaintance with functions, we may still not be able to find the formula for a given antiderivative.

    Aside
    …and there may even be no formula for it
    🔗
    The reason is simple: for most functions we can write down, the antiderivative is just not among the basic functions of calculus. For example, none of the basic functions, in any form or combination, equals
    \begin{equation*} \int e^{x^2} dx \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad \int \dfrac{\sin{x}}{x} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    This does not mean that \(e^{x^2}\text{,}\) for example, has no antiderivative. On the contrary, the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^x e^{t^2} dt \end{equation*}
    is an antiderivative of \(e^{x^2}\text{.}\) It can be evaluated, graphed, and analyzed like any other function. What we lack is a formula for this antiderivative in terms of the basic functions of calculus.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.1.5 Finding Antiderivatives

    In the rest of this chapter we will be deriving a number of statements involving antiderivatives. It is important to remember what such statements mean.
    🔗
    In other words, a statement

    Aside
    We differentiate to verify a statement about antiderivatives
    🔗
    about antiderivatives can be verified by looking at a statement about derivatives. If it is claimed that the antiderivative of \(f\) is \(F\text{,}\) you check the statement by seeing if it is true that \(F'=f\text{.}\) This was how we verified the elementary antiderivatives we’ve considered so far. Another way of expressing these relationships is
    \begin{equation*} \left(\int f(x)\,dx\right)' = f(x)\quad\mbox{and}\quad \int F'(x)\,dx = F(x)+C \end{equation*}
    for any functions \(f\) and \(F\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This duality between statements about derivatives and statements about antiderivatives also holds when applied to more general statements. Many of the most useful techniques for finding antiderivatives are based on converting the general rules for taking derivatives of sums, products, and chains into equivalent antiderivative form.

    Aside
    The constant multiple and addition rules
    🔗
    We’ll start with the simplest combinations, which involve a function multiplied by a constant and a sum of two functions.
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rclcrcl} \multicolumn{3}{c}{\mbox{derivative form}} & & \multicolumn{3}{c}{\mbox{antiderivative form}} \\ [2pt] \hline \\ [-10pt] (k \cdot F)' &=& k \cdot F'& & \dint k \cdot f \, dx &=& k \cdot \!\!\dint f \, dx \\[.15in] (F + G)' &=& F' + G' & \qquad\qquad & \dint (f + g) \, dx &=& \dint f \, dx + \dint g \, dx \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Let’s verify these rules. Set \(\int f=F\) and \(\int g = G\text{.}\) Then the first rule is claiming that \(\int (k\cdot f) = k\cdot F\)—the antiderivative of a constant times a function equals the constant times the antiderivative of the function. To verify this, we have to show that when we take the derivative of the right-hand side, we get the function under the integral on the left-hand side. But \((k\cdot F)'=k\cdot F'\) (by the derivative rule), which is just \(k\cdot f\text{,}\) which is what we had to show.
    🔗
    Similarly, to show that \(\int (f+g) = F + G\)—the antiderivative of the sum of two functions is the sum of their separate antiderivatives—we differentiate the right-hand side and find \((F+G)'=F'+G'\) (by the derivative rule for sums) which is just \(f+g\text{,}\) so the rule is true.
    🔗
    [[EX:3]]This example illustrates the use of both the addition and the constant multiple rules.
    \begin{align*} int (7e^x + \cos{x}) \, dx \amp= \int 7e^x \, dx + \int \cos{x} \, dx\\ \amp= 7 \int e^x \, dx + \int \cos{x} \, dx\\ [6pt] \amp= 7 e^x + \sin{x} + C. \end{align*}
    To verify this answer, you should take the derivative of the right-hand side to see that it equals the integrand on the left-hand side.
    🔗
    In the following sections we will develop the antidifferentiation rules that correspond to the product rule and to the chain rule. They are called integration by parts and integration by substitution, respectively.
    🔗
    While antiderivatives can be hard to find,

    Aside
    Trial and error
    🔗
    they are easy to check. This makes “trial and error” a good strategy. In other words, if you don’t immediately see what the antiderivative should be, but the function doesn’t look too bad, try guessing. When you differentiate your guess, what you see may lead you to a better guess.
    🔗
    [[EX:4]] Find
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = \dint \cos(3x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Since the derivative of \(\sin{u}\) is \(\cos{u}\text{,}\) it is reasonable to try \(\sin(3x)\) as an antiderivative for \(\cos(3x)\text{.}\) Therefore:
    \begin{align*} mbox{\sc first guess: } F(x) \amp= \sin(3x),\\ \mbox{\sc check: } F'(x) \amp= \cos(3x) \cdot 3 \neq \cos(3x). \end{align*}
    We wanted \(F' = \cos(3x)\) but we got \(F' = 3\cos(3x)\text{.}\) The chain rule gave us an extra—and unwanted—factor of 3. We can compensate for that factor by multiplying our first guess by 1/3. Then
    \begin{align*} mbox{\sc second guess: } F(x) \amp= \tfrac{1}{3}\sin(3x),\\ \mbox{\sc check: } F'(x) \amp= \tfrac{1}{3}\cos(3x)\cdot 3 = \cos(3x). \end{align*}
    Thus \(\dint \cos(3x) \, dx = \tfrac{1}{3}\sin(3x)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Because indefinite integrals are difficult to calculate,

    Aside
    Tables of Integrals
    🔗
    reference manuals in mathematics and science often include tables of integrals. There are sometimes many hundreds of individual formulas, organized by the type of function being integrated. A modest selection of such formulas can be found at the back of this book. You should take some time to learn how these tables are arranged and get some practice using them. You should also check some of the more unlikely looking formulas by differentiating them to see that they really are the antiderivatives they are claimed to be.
    🔗
    Computers are having a major impact on integration techniques. We saw in the last chapter that any continuous function—even one given by the output from some laboratory recording device or as the result of a numerical technique like Euler’s method—can be approximated by a polynomial (usually using lots of computation!), and the antiderivative of a polynomial is easy to find.
    🔗
    Moreover, computer software packages

    Aside
    Integration can now be done quickly and efficiently by computer software
    🔗
    which can find any existing formula for a definite integral are becoming widespread and will probably have a profound impact on the importance of integration techniques over the next several years. Just as hand-held calculators have rendered obsolete many traditional arts—like using logarithms for performing multiplications or knowing how to interpolate in trig tables—there is likely to be a decreased importance placed on humans’ being adept at some of the more esoteric integration techniques. While some will continue to derive pleasure from becoming proficient in these skills, for most users it will generally be much faster, and more accurate, to use an appropriate software package. Nevertheless, for those going on in mathematics and the physical sciences, it will still to be useful to be able to perform some of the simpler integrations by hand reasonably rapidly. The subsequent sections of this chapter develop the most commonly needed techniques for doing this.

    23.

    The connection between integration and differentiation that is provided by the fundamental theorem of calculus makes it possible to determine an integral by solving a differential equation. For example, the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} A(x) = \int_0^x e^{-t^2} dt \end{equation*}
    is the solution to the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} y' = e^{-x^2} \, , \qquad y(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    Therefore, \(A(x)\) can be found by solving the differential equation. As you have seen, Euler’s method is a useful way to solve differential equations.
