    Section 12.4 Fourier Series

    In chapter 10.6 we obtained polynomials which were good approximations to a function over an interval, where “good” meant minimizing the mean squared separation between the function and the approximating polynomials.
    🔗
    While polynomials are the most obvious approximating functions to use due to the ease with

    Aside
    Difficulties with polynomial approximations
    🔗
    which they can be evaluated, we have seen that finding good approximating polynomials leads to several serious technical complications. The first is that we have to solve systems of equations to determine the unknown coefficients, a procedure that is very time-consuming, even for a computer if we are trying to get a polynomial of, say, degree 30. Further, if we are trying to make the approximation over even a moderately-sized interval, since we are evaluating expressions of the form \(x^n\text{,}\) we get large numbers very rapidly as \(x\) and \(n\) get large. This in turn leads to roundoff problems in the computer routines.
    🔗
    Another aspect of these polynomial approximations that makes them complicated is that the values of the coefficients change as we change the degree of the approximating polynomial. Thus if we determine the least squares fourth-degree approximation and then decide we want the fifth-degree approximation instead, all the coefficients have to be recalculated. Knowing what the coefficient of \(x^3\) was in the fourth-degree approximation is no help at all in knowing what the coefficient of \(x^3\) will be in the fifth-degree approximation.
    🔗
    There are approximating functions of another kind that avoid such difficulties. Moreover, these functions are natural ones to use

    Aside
    Approximating periodic functions
    🔗
    when we are trying to approximate periodic functions. In such cases it is reasonable to take the simplest periodic functions—sines and cosine—and try to combine them to approximate more complicated periodic functions. This suggests that we want to look at functions of the form
    \begin{align*} phi(x) \amp= a_0 + a_1 \cos{x}+ a_2\cos{2x} + \cdots+ a_n\cos{nx}\\ \amp \mbox{}\quad +b_1 \sin{x} + b_2 \sin{2x} + \cdots + b_n\sin{nx}\\ \amp= a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos{kx} + b_k \sin{kx}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    Such a combination is called a trigonometric polynomial of degree \(n\). Note that any function of this form will in fact be periodic with period \(2\pi\text{.}\) More generally, if we were interested in approximating a function of period \(T\text{,}\) we would want to look at trigonometric polynomials of the form

    Aside
    Trigonometric polynomial of degree \(n\) and period \(T\)
    🔗
    \begin{align*} phi(x) \amp= a_0 +a_1 \cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}+ a_2 \cos\dfrac{4\pi x}{T} + \cdots+ a_n \cos\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T}\\ \amp \mbox{}\quad +b_1 \sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}+ b_2 \sin\dfrac{4\pi x}{T} + \cdots+ b_n \sin\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T}\\ \amp= a_0+\sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} + b_k \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T}. \end{align*}
    You should verify that this does indeed have period \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗
    To find the coefficients \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) of the trigonometric polynomial that best fits a period-\(2 \pi\) function \(f\) over the interval \([c, c + 2 \pi]\text{,}\) we proceed exactly as we did in the previous section, using the least squares criterion. That is, for a given degree \(n\text{,}\) we want to find coefficients \(a_0, \ldots , a_n\) and \(b_1, \ldots , b_n\) that minimize the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_c^{c+2\pi} \left( f(x) -\phi (x)\right)^2\, dx. \end{equation*}
    In practice, \(c\) is usually either 0 or \(-\pi\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The solution turns out to be remarkably compact

    Aside
    Coefficients are independent of \(n\)
    🔗
    and easy to state. One of the key features of the formulas for the coefficients is that they are independent of each other and of the particular value of \(n\) being used. Thus, for example, \(a_3\) in the 7-th degree approximation has the same value as \(a_3\) in the 39-th degree approximation. This is a major advantage compared to the polynomial approximations over intervals that we worked with in chapter 10.
    🔗
    🔗
    The infinite series
    \begin{equation*} a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^{\infty} a_k \cos{kx} + b_k \sin{kx} \end{equation*}
    with the prescribed values for \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) is called the Fourier series for \(f\text{,}\) and the coefficients \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) are called the Fourier coefficients for \(f\text{.}\) It turns out that any continuous function equals its Fourier series in the same sense we used earlier with Taylor series—for any \(x\) in the given interval, \(f(x)\) is the limit as \(n\rightarrow\infty\) of the \(n\)th degree approximating trigonometric polynomials evaluated at \(x\text{.}\) The derivation is straightforward, but we shall leave it to the end of this section so we can look at some examples first.
    🔗

    Aside
    Joseph Fourier (1768–1830) was active in both politics and in mathematics. He was an advocate of the French Revolution, worked as an engineer in Napoleon’s army, and served as a prefect for a while. In mathematics he was interested in the mathematics of heat conduction and developed the series that now bear his name as a tool for investigating problems in this area. His ideas initially met with considerable resistance, but eventually became a central tool in mathematics.
    🔗
    Although our formulas give the values of \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) in terms of integrals over \([0, 2\pi]\text{,}\) periodicity of the integrands implies that integrations over any interval of width \(2\pi\) gives the same values. In practice (as in the first example, immediately below), we often use \([-\pi, \pi]\) instead of \([0, 2\pi]\text{.}\)
    🔗

    The first of these integrals can be evaluated as
    \begin{align*} int_{-\pi}^0 (\pi+x)\cos{kx}\, dx \amp= \left.(\pi+x)\dfrac{\sin{kx}}{k}\, \right|_{-\pi}^0 - \int_{-\pi}^0 \dfrac{\sin{kx}}{k}\, dx\\ \amp= 0 + \left.\dfrac{\cos{kx}}{k^2}\, \right|_{-\pi}^0\\ \amp= \begin{cases} \dfrac{2}{k^2} \amp \quad\text{if $k$ is odd},\\ [6pt] 0 \amp \quad\text{if $k$ is even}. \end{cases} \end{align*}
    Similarly we find
    \begin{align*} int_0^{\pi}(\pi-x)\cos{kx} \, dx \amp= \left.(\pi-x)\dfrac{\sin{kx}}{k}\, \right|_0^{\pi} + \int_0^{\pi} \dfrac{\sin{kx}}{k}\, dx\\ \amp= 0 - \left.\dfrac{\cos{kx}}{k^2}\, \right|_0^{\pi}\\ \amp=\begin{cases} \dfrac{2}{k^2} \amp \quad\text{if $k$ is odd},\\ [6pt] 0 \amp \quad\text{if $k$ is even}. \end{cases} \end{align*}
    🔗
    Combining these two integrals we find
    \begin{equation*} a_k = \begin{cases} \dfrac{4}{\pi k^2} & \quad\text{if $k$ is odd}, \\[6pt] \hphantom{0}0 & \quad\text{if $k$ is even}. \end{cases} \end{equation*}

    Remark 2In addition to their use in approximating functions, Fourier series can lead to some interesting, and non-obvious, mathematical results. For instance in the preceding example, we have \(f(0)=\pi\) . On the other hand, we should get the same value if we set \(x=0\) in the Fourier series for \(f\text{.}\) This leads to the identity
    \begin{equation*} \pi = \dfrac{\pi}{2} + \dfrac{4}{\pi} \left( \dfrac{1}{1} + \dfrac{1}{9} + \dfrac{1}{25} + \dfrac{1}{49} + \dfrac{1}{81} + \cdots \right). \end{equation*}
    With a little rearranging, this can be rewritten as
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\pi^2}{8} = 1 + \dfrac{1}{9} + \dfrac{1}{25} + \dfrac{1}{49} + \dfrac{1}{81} + \cdots \end{equation*}
    —that is, if we add up the reciprocals of the squares of all the odd integers, we get \(\pi^2/8\text{!}\)

    Fourier series for the periodic solutions of May’s predator-prey model
    🔗
    predator–prey model of May that we examined in chapter 7.3. Recall that there were two species, the predator \(y\) and the prey \(x\text{,}\) interacting according to the model
    \begin{align*} mbox{prey:} \quad x' \amp= .6\,x\left(1-\frac{x}{10}\right) - \frac{.5\,xy}{x+1},\\ \mbox{predator:} \quad y' \amp= .1\,y\left(1-\frac{y}{2x}\right). \end{align*}
    We discovered that the populations seemed to move towards periodic cycles, regardless of the initial conditions (although the phase of the cycles did depend on the starting values). In particular, if we begin with values on this cycle, our solution should be perfectly periodic with period, it turned out, \(T=38.6\) days. So let’s start with \(x=7.75\) and \(y=2.38\text{,}\) values that put us at the peak of the prey cycle. Now go to the differential equations and compute the solution numerically, storing the \(x\)-values as an array. We can then use these values to calculate all the integrals needed to find the Fourier coefficients to approximate the function \(x(t)\text{.}\) Here are the first 13 terms of the series:
    \begin{align*} x(t)\amp= 3.7951 + 3.8125\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{T} + .1514\cos\dfrac{4\pi x}{T} + .0326\cos\dfrac{6\pi x}{T}\\ [6pt] \amp \mbox{}\quad - .0303\cos\dfrac{8\pi x}{T} - .0609\cos\dfrac{10\pi x}{T}+.0308\cos\dfrac{12\pi x}{T} + \cdots\\ [8pt] \amp \mbox{}\quad + 1.1724\sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{T} - .0867\sin\dfrac{4\pi x}{T}-.3954\sin\dfrac{6\pi x}{T}\\ [8pt] \amp \mbox{}\quad + .0639\sin\dfrac{8\pi x}{T} - .0142\sin\dfrac{10\pi x}{T}+.0129\sin\dfrac{12\pi x}{T} + \cdots . \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let \(\phi_3(t)\) be the 7-term trigonometric polynomial whose final terms involve \(\cos(6\pi t/T)\) and \(\sin(6\pi t/T)\text{.}\) Below we graph \(\phi_3(t)\) (dashed line) and \(x(t)\) (solid gray line) together. They are almost indistinguishable; we let \(\phi_3(t)\) run on a little beyond \(x(t)\) so you can see it’s there.
    🔗

    Derivation of the formula for the Fourier coefficients.

    The logic behind the derivation is the same as that used in the previous subsection to find the least squares polynomial approximations. Fix \(n\) and let
    \begin{equation*} \phi(x) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} + b_k \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T}, \end{equation*}
    where now we want to choose values of the \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) to minimize the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^T \left( f(x) -\phi (x)\right)^2\, dx. \end{equation*}
    The value of this integral is thus a function of the undetermined coefficients \(a_0, \ldots , a_n\) and \(b_1, \ldots , b_n\text{.}\) To find the coefficients that minimize that value we calculate the partial derivatives with respect to \(a_0\text{,}\) \(a_1\text{,}\) …as before and set them equal to 0.
    🔗
    Note that
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial a_m}\phi(x) = \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \quad \mbox{and} \quad \dfrac{\partial}{\partial b_m}\phi(x) = \sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T}, \end{equation*}
    so that
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial a_m} \int_0^T (f(x) -\phi (x))^2 \, dx = \int_0^T 2(f(x) -\phi (x)) \left(-\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T}\right)\, dx \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\partial}{\partial b_m} \int_0^T (f(x) -\phi (x))^2 \, dx = \int_0^T 2 (f(x) -\phi (x)) \left(-\sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T}\right)\, dx. \end{equation*}
    The condition that all the partial derivatives must be 0 thus leads to the equations
    \begin{align*} int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x))(-1) \, dx \amp= 0,\\ [3pt] \int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0,\\ [6pt] \int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\cos\dfrac{4\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0,\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-5pt] \int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\cos\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0, \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{align*} int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0,\\ [8pt] \int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\sin\dfrac{4\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0,\\ [-3pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-3pt] \int_0^T 2 (f(x) - \phi(x)) \left(-\sin\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T}\right) \, dx \amp= 0. \end{align*}
    These equations can be rewritten as
    \begin{align*} int_0^T f(x) \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\, dx,\\ [10pt] \int_0^T f(x)\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}\, dx,\\ [10pt] \int_0^T f(x)\cos\dfrac{4\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{4\pi x}{T}\, dx,\\ [-3pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-3pt] \int_0^T f(x)\cos\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T}\, dx, \end{align*}
    and
    \begin{align*} int_0^T f(x)\sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{2\pi x}{T}\, dx,\\ [10pt] \int_0^T f(x)\sin\dfrac{4\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{4\pi x}{T}\, dx,\\ [-3pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-3pt] \int_0^T f(x)\sin\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \int_0^T \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{2n\pi x}{T} \, dx, \end{align*}
    🔗
    The Fourier coefficients \(a_k\) and \(b_k\) that we seek appear in \(\phi\text{,}\) and we shall obtain them by calculating the integrals on the right (the ones involving \(\phi\)) in the equations above. Since
    \begin{equation*} \phi(x) = a_0 + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T}, \end{equation*}
    we have (for each \(m = 0, 1, \ldots , n\))
    \begin{align*} phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \amp= a_0 \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} + \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T}\\ \amp\mbox{}\quad + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T},\\ \intertext{and (for each $m = 1, \ldots , n$)} \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \amp= \sum_{k=1}^n a_k \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} + \sum_{k=1}^n b_k \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Aside
    Integrating products of sines and cosines
    🔗
    therefore reduce to sums of integrals of various products of sines and cosines. In each sum, only one term yields a nonzero integral. All the values are given below. The formulas are left for you to derive in the exercises, using integration formulas from the exercises in chapter 11.3. For integers \(k\) and \(m\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} int_0^T \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= 0 \quad \mbox{ for all $k$ and $m$};\\ [6pt] \int_0^T \sin\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \begin{cases} T/2 \amp \text{if $k=m$},\\ 0 \amp \text{otherwise}; \end{cases}\\ [8pt] \int_0^T \cos\dfrac{2k\pi x}{T} \cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx \amp= \begin{cases} T/2 \amp \text{if $k=m \ne 0$},\\ T \amp \text{if $k=m=0$},\\ 0 \amp \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \end{align*}
    For \(m = 0\) we have \(\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} = \cos{0} = 1\text{,}\) so
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx = \int_0^T a_0 \ dx = a_0 \cdot T; \end{equation*}
    it follows that
    \begin{equation*} a_0 = \dfrac{1}{T} \int_0^T \phi(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    For each \(m = 1, 2, \ldots , n\text{,}\) we have, first of all,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx = \int_0^T a_m \cos^2\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx = a_m \cdot T/2, \end{equation*}
    from which it follows that
    \begin{equation*} a_m = \dfrac{2}{T} \int_0^T \phi(x)\cos\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Second, we have
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^T \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx = \int_0^T b_m \sin^2\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx = b_m \cdot T/2, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} b_m = \dfrac{2}{T} \int_0^T \phi(x)\sin\dfrac{2m\pi x}{T} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    The derivation is complete.
