Recall that the rate \(S'\) (or \(I'\) or \(R'\)) is simply the multiplier which gives \(\Delta S\)—the (estimated) change in \(S\)—for a given change \(\Delta t\) in \(t\)
\begin{equation*} \Delta S = S'\cdot \Delta t\, . \end{equation*}
This relation holds for any value of \(\Delta t\text{,}\) integer or not. Once we have this value for \(\Delta S\text{,}\) we can then calculate
\begin{equation*} \mbox{new (estimated) } S = \mbox{current (estimated) } S + \Delta S \end{equation*}
in the usual way. Note that we have written “(estimated)” throughout to emphasize the fact that if \(S'\) is not constant over the entire time \(\Delta t\text{,}\) then the value we get for \(\Delta S\) will typically be only an approximation to the real change in \(S\text{.}\)
🔗

A second feature is that as we read down the column under \(S(1)\text{,}\) we find each digit stabilizes—that is, after changing for a while, it eventually becomes fixed at a particular value. The initial digits 44 are the first to stabilize, and that happens by the time \(\Delta t = 0.1\text{.}\) Then the third digit 2 stabilizes, when \(\Delta t = 0.01\text{.}\) Roughly speaking, one more digit stabilizes at each successive level. The table is revealing to us, digit by digit, the true value of \(S(1)\text{.}\) By the fifth stage we learn that the integer part of \(S(1)\) is 44255. By the sixth stage we can say that the true value of \(S(1)\) is \(44255.4\ldots\) .

When we write \(S(1) = 44255.4\ldots\)

Aside
Approximations lead to exact values
🔗
we are expressing \(S(1)\) to one decimal place accuracy. This says, first, that the decimal expansion of \(S(1)\) begins with exactly the six digits shown and, second, that there are further digits after the 4 (represented by the three dots “…”). In this case, we can identify further digits simply by continuing the table. Since our step sizes have the form \(\Delta t = 10^{-N}\text{,}\) we just need to increase \(N\text{.}\) For example, to express \(S(1)\) accurately to six decimal places, we need to stabilize the first eleven digits in our estimates of \(S(1)\text{.}\) The table suggests that \(\Delta t\) should probably be about \(10^{-10}\)—i.e., \(N = 10\text{.}\)
🔗
The true value of \(S(1)\) emerges through a process that generates a sequence of successive approximations. We say \(S(1) = 44255.4\)…is the limit of this sequence as \(\Delta t\) is made smaller and smaller or, equivalently, as \(N\) is made larger and larger. We also say that the sequence of successive approximations converges to the limit \(S(1)\text{.}\) Here is a mathematical notation that expresses these statements more compactly:
\begin{align*} S(1) \amp= \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \{\mbox{the estimate of $S(1)$}\} \qquad \mbox{or, equivalently,}\\ \amp= \lim_{N \to \infty} \{\mbox{the estimate of $S(1)$ with $\Delta t = 10^{-N}$}\}. \end{align*}
The symbol \(\infty\) stands for “infinity,” and the expression \(N \to \infty\) is often pronounced “as \(N\) goes to infinity.” However, it is often more instructive to say “as \(N\) gets larger and larger, without bound.”

Aside
The limit concept lies at the heart of calculus. Later on we’ll give a precise definition, but you should first see limits at work in a number of contexts and begin to develop some intuitions about what they are. This approach mirrors the historical development of calculus—mathematicians freely used limits for well over a century before a careful, rigorous definition was developed.
