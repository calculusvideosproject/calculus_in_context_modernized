    Section 11.4 Separation of Variables andPartial Fractions

    One of the principal uses of integration techniques is to find closed form solutions to differential equations. If you look back at the methods we have developed so far in this chapter, they are all applicable to differential equations of the form \(y'=f(t)\) for some function \(f\)—that is, the rate at which \(y\) changes is a function of the independent variable only. In such cases we only need to find an antiderivative \(F\) for \(f\text{,}\) choose the constant \(C\) to satisfy the initial value, and we have our solution. As we saw in the early chapters, though, the behavior of \(y'\) often depends on the values of \(y\) rather than on \(t\)—think of the \(S\)-\(I\)-\(R\) model or the various predator-prey problems. In this section we will see how our earlier techniques can be adapted to apply to problems of this sort as well.
    🔗

    Subsection 11.4.1 The Differential Equation \(y' = y\)

    As you know,

    Aside
    A new method for solving \(y' = y\)
    🔗
    the exponential functions \(y = C e^t\) are the solutions to the differential equation \(dy/dt = y\text{.}\) Let’s put aside this knowledge for a moment and rediscover these solutions using a new method. The method involves the connection between inverse functions and their derivatives. With it we will be able to explore a variety of problems that had been beyond our reach.
    🔗
    The idea behind the new method is quite simple:

    Aside
    Find the inverse function instead
    🔗
    Instead of thinking of \(y\) as a function of \(t\text{,}\) convert to thinking of \(t\) as a function of \(y\text{,}\) thereby looking for the inverse function. We know that the derivative of the inverse function is the reciprocal of the derivative of the original function, so we can rewrite the given differential equation by using its reciprocal:

    Aside
    The new differential equation…
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = y \qquad \mbox{becomes} \qquad \dfrac{dt}{dy} = \dfrac{1}{y}. \end{equation*}
    Then solve the new differential equation \(dt/dy = 1/y\text{.}\) While this may not look very different, it has the property that the rate of change of the dependent variable—now \(t\)—is expressed as a function of the independent variable—now \(y\text{.}\) But this is just the form we have been considering in the earlier sections of this chapter. A solution to the new equation is a function \(t = g(y)\) whose derivative is \(1/y\text{.}\) This is one of the basic antiderivatives listed

    Aside
    …and its solution
    🔗
    in the table on page :
    \begin{equation*} t = g(y) = \ln{y} + k, \end{equation*}
    where \(k\) is an arbitrary constant.
    🔗
    The solution to the original differential equation

    Aside
    The inverse …
    🔗
    \(dy/dt = y\) is the inverse of \(t = \ln{y} + k\text{.}\) We find it by solving this equation for \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} t - k \amp= \ln{y}\\ e^{t - k} \amp= y\\ e^t \cdot e^{-k} \amp= y \end{align*}
    Thus \(y(t) = Ce^t\text{,}\)

    Aside
    …solves the original problem
    🔗
    where we have replaced the constant \(e^{-k}\) by \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Indefinite integrals.

    The language of indefinite integrals

    Aside
    The differential equation expressed using differentials
    🔗
    and differentials again provides a convenient mnemonic for this new method. First, we use the original differential equation to relate the differentials \(dy\) and \(dt\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} dy = \dfrac{dy}{dt} \, dt = y \, dt. \end{equation*}
    This use of differentials is introduced on page . At this point the equation makes sense for either \(t\) or \(y\) being the independent variable. Now if we try to integrate this equation with respect to \(t\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} y = \int dy = \int y(t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    We can’t find the last integral, because we don’t know what \(y\) is as a function of \(t\text{.}\) Remember, an indefinite integral is an antiderivative, so an expression of the form
    \begin{equation*} \int f \, dt \end{equation*}
    represents a function \(F(t)\) whose derivative is \(f(t)\text{.}\) If \(f\) is not given by a formula in \(t\text{,}\) there is no way to get a formula for \(F(t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose, though, that we divide both sides of the differential equation \(dy = y \, dt\) by \(y\text{,}\) and integrate with respect to \(y\text{.}\) The equation takes the form
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{y} = dt. \end{equation*}
    Now, if we introduce indefinite integrals, we have
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dy}{y} = \int dt. \end{equation*}
    This time the variables \(y\) and \(t\)

    Aside
    The variables are now separated
    🔗
    have been separated from each other, and we can find the integrals. In fact,
    \begin{equation*} \ln{y} = \int \dfrac{dy}{y} = \int dt = t + b, \end{equation*}
    where \(b\) is an arbitrary constant. (We could just as easily have added the constant to the left side instead—do you see why we don’t have to add a constant to both sides?) To complete the work

    Aside
    The solution once again
    🔗
    we solve for \(y\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = e^{t + b} = e^t \cdot e^b = Ce^t, \end{equation*}
    where \(C = e^b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Summary.

    The first time we went through the method, we replaced
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = y \qquad \mbox{by} \qquad \dfrac{dt}{dy} = \dfrac{1}{y}. \end{equation*}
    These differential equations express the same relation between \(y\) and \(t\text{.}\) Each is just the reciprocal of the other. In the first, \(y\) depends on \(t\text{;}\) in the second, though, \(t\) depends on \(y\text{.}\) The second time we went through the method, using indefinite integrals, we replaced
    \begin{equation*} dy = y \, dt \qquad \mbox{by} \qquad dt = \dfrac{dy}{y}. \end{equation*}
    This change was also algebraic, and it had the same effect: the dependent variable changed from \(y\) to \(t\text{.}\) More important,

    Aside
    To integrate, separate the variables
    🔗
    in the new differential equation (using the differentials themselves!) the variables are separated. That allows us to do the integration. We get a solution in the form \(t = g(y)\text{.}\) The solution to the original problem is the inverse \(y = f(t)\) of \(t = g(y)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.4.2 Separation of Variables

    With the method of separation of variables, introduced in the previous pages, we can obtain formulas for solutions to a number of differential equations that were previously accessible only by Euler’s method. Recall that one of the clear advantages of a formula is that it allows us to see how the parameters in the problem affect the solution. We’ll look at two problems. First we’ll show how the method can explain the rather baffling formula for supergrowth that we gave in chapter 4. Then, using the method of partial fractions, to be discussed next, we’ll give a formula for logistic growth.
    🔗

    Supergrowth.

    In chapter 4.2 we modelled the growth of a population \(Q\) by the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dQ}{dt} = k Q^{1.2}, \qquad Q(0) = A. \end{equation*}
    To get a formula for the solution,

    Aside
    Separate the variables
    🔗
    transform the differential equation in the following way:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dQ}{dt} = k \, Q^{1.2} \quad \leadsto \quad dQ = k \, Q^{1.2} \, dt \quad \leadsto \quad \dfrac{dQ}{Q^{1.2}} = k \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Now integrate:
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dQ}{Q^{1.2}} = \int k \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Because the variables have been separated, the integrals can be found:
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dQ}{Q^{1.2}} \amp= \int Q^{-1.2} \, dQ = \dfrac{1}{-.2} Q^{-.2}\\ [3pt] \dint k \, dt \amp= kt + C \end{align*}
    Therefore, \(\tfrac{1}{-.2} Q^{-.2} = kt + C\text{.}\)

    Aside
    Solve for \(Q\)
    🔗
    Now we must solve this equation for \(Q\text{.}\) Here is one possible approach. First, we can write
    \begin{equation*} Q^{-.2} = -.2(kt + C) \ = \ C_1 -.2kt. \end{equation*}
    To simplify the expression, we have replaced \(-.2C\) by a new constant \(C_1\text{.}\) Since
    \begin{equation*} (Q^{-.2})^{-5} = Q^{-.2 \times -5} = Q^1 = Q, \end{equation*}
    we’ll raise both sides of the previous equation to the power \(-5\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q(t) = (Q^{-.2})^{-5} = (C_1 -.2kt)^{-5}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The last step is to incorporate

    Aside
    Bring in the initial condition
    🔗
    the initial condition \(Q(0) = A\text{.}\) According to the new formula for \(Q(t)\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} Q(0) = (C_1 -.2k\cdot 0)^{-5} = C_1^{-5} = A. \end{equation*}
    Solving \(A = C_1^{-5}\) for \(C_1\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} C_1 = A^{-1/5} = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[5]{A}}. \end{equation*}
    We are now done:
    \begin{equation*} Q(t) = \left(\dfrac{1}{\sqrt[5]{A}} -.2kt \right)^{-5}. \end{equation*}
    This is the formula that appears on page .

    Aside
    Interpreting the formula
    🔗
    It shows how the parameters \(k\) and \(A\) affect the solution. In particular, we called this supergrowth because the model predicts that the population \(Q\) becomes infinite when
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt[5]{A}} -.2kt = 0; \quad \mbox{that is, when} \quad t = \dfrac{1}{.2k \sqrt[5]{A}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.4.3 Partial Fractions

    Using separation of variables with a partial fractions decomposition (to be described below), we will obtain a formula for the solution to the logistic equation (chapter 4.1). The method of partial fractions is a useful tool for solving many integration problems.
    🔗

    Logistic growth.

    Consider this initial value problem associated with the logistic differential equation:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP \left( 1 - \dfrac{P}{C} \right), \qquad P(0) = A. \end{equation*}
    We will find a formula for the solution that incorporates the growth parameter \(k\) and the carrying capacity \(C\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The first step is to transform the equation

    Aside
    Step 1: separate the variables
    🔗
    into one where the variables are separated:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dP}{dt} = kP \left( 1 - \dfrac{P}{C} \right) \quad \leadsto \quad \dfrac{dP}{P (1 - P/C)} = k \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Integrating the new equation we get
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dP}{P (1 - P/C)} = \int k \, dt. \end{equation*}
    We are stuck now,

    Aside
    The denominator has an unfamiliar form
    🔗
    because the integral on the left doesn’t appear in our table of integrals (page ). If the denominator had only \(P\) or only \(1 - P/C\text{,}\) we could use the natural logarithm. The difficulty is that the denominator is the product of both terms.
    🔗
    There is a way out of the difficulty. We will use algebra to transform the integrand into a form we can work with. The first step is to simplify the denominator a bit:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{P(1 - P/C)} = \dfrac{1}{P(C/C - P/C)} = \dfrac{1}{P(C - P)/C} = \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)}. \end{equation*}
    (This wasn’t essential; it just makes later steps easier to write.) The next step will be the crucial one. To understand why we take it, consider the rule for adding two fractions:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\alpha}{(x + a)} + \dfrac{\beta}{(x + b)} = \dfrac{\alpha (x + b) + \beta (x + a)}{(x + a) (x + b)} \end{equation*}
    The denominator is a product—very much like the product \(P(C - P)\)

    Aside
    Step 2: write the integrand as a sum of simple fractions
    🔗
    in our integrand! Perhaps we can write that as a sum of two simpler fractions:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)} = \dfrac{\alpha}{P} + \dfrac{\beta}{C - P}. \end{equation*}
    What values should \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) have? According to the rule for adding fractions,
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\alpha}{P} + \dfrac{\beta}{C - P} = \dfrac{\alpha (C - P) + \beta P}{P(C - P)}, \end{equation*}
    and this should equal the original integrand:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\alpha (C - P) + \beta P}{P(C - P)} = \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since the denominators are equal,

    Aside
    Determining \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\)
    🔗
    the numerators must also be equal:
    \begin{equation*} \alpha (C - P) + \beta P = C. \end{equation*}
    In fact, they must be equal as polynomials in the variable \(P\text{.}\) If we rewrite the last equation, collecting terms that involve the same power of \(P\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} (\beta - \alpha)P + \alpha C = 0 \cdot P + 1 \cdot C. \end{equation*}
    Since two polynomials are equal precisely when their coefficients are equal, it follows that
    \begin{equation*} \beta - \alpha = 0 \qquad \alpha = 1. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\alpha = \beta = 1\text{,}\)

    Aside
    The partial fractions decomposition
    🔗
    and we have
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)} = \dfrac{1}{P} + \dfrac{1}{C - P}. \end{equation*}
    The simpler expressions on the right are called partial fractions. Their denominators are the different parts of the denominator of the integrand. The equation that expresses the integrand as a sum of partial fractions is called a partial fractions decomposition.
    🔗
    We can now return to the integral equation we are trying to solve:
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)} \, dP = \int k \, dt. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The right hand side equals \(kt + b\text{,}\)

    Aside
    Step 3: evaluate the integrals
    🔗
    where \(b\) is the usual constant of integration. Thanks to the partial fractions decomposition, the left hand side can be written
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{C}{P(C - P)} \, dP = \int \dfrac{1}{P} \, dP + \int \dfrac{1}{C - P} \, dP. \end{equation*}
    The first integral on the right is straightforward:
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{1}{P} \, dP = \ln{P}. \end{equation*}
    The second can be solved by using the substitution \(C - P = u\text{,}\) with \(dP = -du\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{1}{C - P} \, dP = \int \dfrac{-du}{u} = - \ln{u} = -\ln(C - P). \end{equation*}
    Putting everything together we find
    \begin{equation*} \ln{P} - \ln(C - P) = \ln \left( \dfrac{P}{C - P} \right) = kt + b. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As we have seen,

    Aside
    Step 4: solve for \(P\)
    🔗
    separation of variables usually leaves us with an inverse function to find. This problem is no different. We must solve the last equation for \(P\text{.}\) The first step is to exponentiate both sides:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{P}{C - P} = e^{kt + b} = e^b \cdot e^{kt} = Be^{kt}. \end{equation*}
    To simplify the expression a bit, we have replaced \(e^b\) by \(B = e^b\text{.}\) Multiplying both sides by \(C - P\) gives
    \begin{equation*} P = Be^{kt}(C - P) = CBe^{kt} - PBe^{kt}. \end{equation*}
    Now bring the last term over to the left, and then factor out \(P\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} P + PBe^{kt} = \left( 1 + Be^{kt} \right) P = CBe^{kt}. \end{equation*}
    The final step is to divide

    Aside
    The formula for \(P(t)\)
    🔗
    by the coefficient \(1 + Be^{kt}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = \dfrac{CBe^{kt}}{1 + Be^{kt}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Lastly,

    Aside
    Step 5: incorporate the initial condition
    🔗
    we must see how the initial condition \(P(0) = A\) affects the solution. We could substitute \(t = 0\text{,}\) \(P = A\) into the last formula, but that produces an algebraic mess. We want to know how \(A\) affects the constant \(B\text{,}\) and we can see that directly by making our substitutions into the equation above:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{P}{C - P} = Be^{kt} \quad \leadsto \quad \dfrac{A}{C - A} = Be^0 = B. \end{equation*}
    Now replace \(B\) by \(A/(C - A)\) in our formula for \(P(t)\text{.}\) This yields
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = \dfrac{CAe^{kt}/(C - A)}{1 + Ae^{kt}/(C - A)} = \dfrac{CAe^{kt}}{C - A + Ae^{kt}} \end{equation*}
    If we write \(-A + Ae^{kt} = A(e^{kt} - 1)\text{,}\) we get one of the standard forms of the solution to

    Aside
    The complete solution
    🔗
    the logistic equation:
    \begin{equation*} P(t) = \dfrac{CAe^{kt}}{C + A(e^{kt} - 1)}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Remark..

    The method of partial fractions can be used to evaluate integrals of the form
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{(x + a_1) (x + a_2) \cdots (x + a_n)} \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad \int \dfrac{P(x)}{Q(x)} \, dx, \end{equation*}
    where \(P(x)\) and \(Q(x)\) are arbitrary polynomials. Tables of integrals and calculus references describe how the method works in these cases. As one example, though, let’s compute the antiderivative of the cosecant function, since we will need it in the next section.
    🔗
    Example—The antiderivative of the cosecant We first use a trigonometric identity to transform the integral slightly:
    \begin{align*} dint \csc{x}\,dx \amp= \dint \dfrac{1}{\sin{x}}\,dx\\ [6pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{\sin{x}}{\sin^2{x}}\,dx\\ [6pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{\sin{x}\,dx}{1-\cos^2{x}}. \end{align*}
    If we now make the substitution \(u=\cos{x}\text{,}\) with \(du=-\sin{x}\,dx\text{,}\) this becomes
    \begin{align*} dint \csc{x}\,dx \amp= \dint \dfrac{-du}{1-u^2}\\ [3pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{-1}{2}\left(\dfrac{1}{1+u} + \dfrac{1}{1-u}\right)\,du\\ [3pt] \amp= -\dfrac{1}{2}\dint \dfrac{du}{1+u} - \dfrac{1}{2}\dint \dfrac{du}{1-u}\\ [3pt] \amp= -\dfrac{1}{2}\ln(1+u) + \dfrac{1}{2} \ln(1-u) +C\\ [6pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\dfrac{1-u}{1+u}} +C\\ [6pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}\ln{\dfrac{1-\cos{x}}{1+\cos{x}}}+C. \end{align*}
    We can simplify this slightly

    Aside
    The final form of the antiderivative of the cosecant
    🔗
    by multiplying both numerator and denominator by \(1+\cos{x}\) to get
    \begin{align*} dint \csc{x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}\ln\dfrac{1-\cos^2{x}}{(1+\cos{x})^2}+C = \ln{\left|\dfrac{\sin{x}}{1+\cos{x}}\right|}+C\\ \amp=-\ln{|\csc{x}+\cot{x}|}+C.\label{cosecant} \end{align*}
    Note that in the next to last line we used the general fact about logarithms that \(n\,\ln{A}=\ln(A^n)\) for any value of \(n\) and any \(A>0\text{.}\) Note also that since the domain of the secant function consists of infinitely many separate intervals. the “\(+\ C\)” at the end of the antiderivative needs to be interpreted as potentially a different value of \(C\) over each interval.
    🔗
    In the same fashion we can

    Aside
    The antiderivative of the secant
    🔗
    obtain the antiderivative for the secant function:
    \begin{equation*} \int \sec{x}\,dx=\ln{|\sec{x} +\tan{x}|}+C. \end{equation*}
