    Section 7.2 Period, Frequency, andthe Circular Functions

    We are familiar with the notions of period and frequency from everyday experience. For example, a full moon occurs every 28 days, which means that a lunar cycle has a period of 28 days and a frequency of once per 28 days. Moreover, whatever phase the moon is in today, it will be in the same phase 28 days from now. Let’s see how to extend these notions to functions.
    🔗
    The function \(y = g(x)\) whose graph is

    Aside
    Defining a periodic function
    🔗
    sketched below has a pattern that repeats. The space \(T\) between one high point and the next tells us the period of this repeating pattern. There is nothing special about the high point, though. If we take any two points \(x\) and \(x + T\) that are spaced one period apart, we find that \(g\) has the same value at those point.
    🔗
    (This is analogous to saying that the moon is in the same phase on any two days that are 28 days apart.) The condition \(g(x + T) = g(x)\) for every \(x\) guarantees that \(g\) will be periodic. We make it the basis of our definition.
    🔗
    Since the graph of \(g\) repeats after \(x\) increases by \(T\text{,}\)

    Aside
    A periodic function has many periods…
    🔗
    it also repeats after \(x\) increases by \(2T\text{,}\) or \(-3T\text{,}\) or any integer multiple (positive or negative) of \(T\text{.}\) This means that a periodic function always has many periods. (That’s why the definition refers to “a period” rather than “the period.”) The same is true of the moon; its phases also repeat

    Aside
    …but we call the smallest positive one the period
    🔗
    after \(2 \times 28\) days, or \(3 \times 28\text{,}\) days. Nevertheless, we think of 28 days as the period of the lunar cycle, because we see the entire pattern precisely once. We can say the same for any periodic function:
    🔗
    Another measure of a periodic function is its

    Aside
    Frequency
    🔗
    frequency. Consider first the lunar cycle. Its frequency is the number of cycles—or fractions of a cycle—that occur in unit time. If we measure time in days, then the frequency is 1/28-th of a cycle per day. If we measure time in years, though, then the frequency is about 13 cycles per year. Here is the calculation:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{365 \ \mbox{days/year}}{28 \ \mbox{days/cycle}} \approx 13 \ \mbox{cycles/year}. \end{equation*}
    Using this example as a pattern, we make the following definition.
    🔗
    Notice that the period and the frequency of the lunar cycle

    Aside
    The frequency of a cycle is the reciprocal of its period
    🔗
    are reciprocals: the period is 28 days—the time needed to complete one cycle—while the frequency is 1/28-th of a cycle per day. In the example below, \(t\) is measured in seconds and \(g\) has a period of .2 seconds. Its frequency is therefore 5 cycles per second.
    🔗
    In general, if \(f\) is the frequency of a periodic function \(g(x)\) and \(T\) is its period, then we have
    \begin{equation*} f = \dfrac{1}{T} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad T = \dfrac{1}{f}. \end{equation*}
    The units are also related in a reciprocal fashion: if the period is measured in seconds, then the frequency is measured in cycles per second.
    🔗
    Because many quantities fluctuate periodically over time, the input variable of a periodic function will often be time.

    Aside
    The units for measuring frequency over time
    🔗
    If time is measured in seconds, then frequency is measured in “cycles per second.” The term Hertz is a special unit used to measure time frequencies; it equals one cycle per second. Hertz is abbreviated Hz; thus a kilohertz (kHz) and a megahertz (MHz) are 1,000 and 1,000,000 cycles per second, respectively. This unit is commonly used to describe sound, light, radio, and television waves. For example, an orchestra tunes to an A at 440 Hz. If an FM radio station broadcasts at 88.5 MHz, this means its carrier frequency is 88,500,000 cycles per second.
    🔗
    Quantities may also be periodic in other dimensions. For instance, a scientist studying the

    Aside
    Functions can be periodic over other units as well
    🔗
    phenomenon of ripple formation in a river bed might be interested in the function \(h(x)\) measuring the height of a ripple as a function of its distance \(x\) along the river bed. This would lead to a function of period, say, 10 inches and corresponding frequency of .1 cycle per inch.
    🔗
    Circular functions. While there are innumerable examples of periodic functions, two in particular are considered basic: the sine and the cosine. They are called circular functions because they are defined by means of a circle. To be specific, take the circle of radius 1 centered at the origin in the \(x, y\)-plane. Given any real number \(t\text{,}\) measure a distance of \(t\) units around the circumference of the circle. Start on the positive \(x\)-axis, and measure
    🔗
    counterclockwise if \(t\) is positive, clockwise if \(t\) is negative. The coordinates of the point you reach this way are, by definition, the cosine and the sine functions of \(t\text{,}\) respectively:
    \begin{align*} x \amp= \cos(t),\\ y \amp= \sin(t). \end{align*}
    🔗
    The whole circumference of the circle measures \(2\pi\) units.

    Aside
    Why \(\cos{t}\) and \(\sin{t}\) are periodic
    🔗
    Therefore, if we add \(2\pi\) units to the \(t\) units we have already measured, we will arrive back at the same point on the circle. That is, we get to the same point on the circle by measuring either \(t\) or \(t + 2\pi\) units around the circumference. We can describe the coordinates of this point two ways:
    \begin{equation*} (\cos(t), \sin(t)) \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad (\cos(t + 2\pi), \sin(t + 2\pi)). \end{equation*}
    Thus
    \begin{equation*} \cos(t + 2\pi) = \cos(t) \qquad \sin(t + 2\pi) = \sin(t), \end{equation*}
    so \(\cos(t)\) and \(\sin(t)\) are both periodic, and they have the same period, \(2\pi\text{.}\) Here are their graphs. By reading their slopes we can see \((\sin{t})' = \cos{t}\) and \((\cos{t})' = -\sin{t}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The circular functions are constructed

    Aside
    Radian measure
    🔗
    without reference to angles; the variable \(t\) is measured around the circumference of a circle (of radius 1). Nevertheless, we can think of \(t\) as measuring an angle, as shown at the right. In this case, \(t\) is called the radian measure of the angle. The units are very different from the degree measurement of an angle: an angle of 1 radian is much larger than an angle of 1 degree. The radian measure of a 90\(^\circ\) angle is \(\pi/2 \approx 1.57\text{,}\) for instance. If we thought of \(t\) as an angle measured in degrees, the slope of \(\sin(t)\) would equal \(.017\cos{t}\text{!}\) (See the exercises.) Only when we measure \(t\) in radians do we get a simple result: \((\sin{t})' = \cos{t}\text{.}\) This is why we always measure angles in radians in calculus.
    🔗
    Compare the graph of \(y = \sin(t)\) above

    Aside
    Changing the frequency
    🔗
    with that of \(y = \sin(4t)\text{,}\) below.
    🔗
    Their scales are identical, so it is clear that the frequency of \(\sin(4t)\) is four times the frequency of \(\sin(t)\text{.}\) The general pattern is described in the following table.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccc} \mbox{function} & \mbox{period} & \mbox{frequency} \\[2pt] \hline \\ [-9pt] \sin(t) \quad \ \cos(t) & 2\pi & 1/2\pi\\ \sin(4t)\quad \cos(4t) & 2\pi/4 & 4/2\pi\\ \sin(bt)\quad \cos(bt) & 2\pi/b & b/2\pi \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Notice that it is the frequency—not the period—that is increased by a factor of \(b\) when we multiply the input variable by \(b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    By using the information in the table,

    Aside
    Constructing a circular function with a given frequency
    🔗
    we can construct circular functions with any period or frequency whatsoever. For instance, suppose we wanted a cosine function \(x = \cos(bt)\) with a frequency of 5 cycles per unit \(t\text{.}\) This means
    \begin{equation*} 5 = \mbox{frequency} = \dfrac{b}{2\pi}, \end{equation*}
    which implies that we should set \(b = 10\pi\) and \(x = \cos(10\pi t)\text{.}\) In order to see the high-frequency behavior of this function better, we magnify the graph a bit. In the figure below, you can compare the graphs of \(x = \cos(10 \pi t)\) and \(x = \cos(t)\) directly. We still have equal scales on the horizontal and vertical axes. Finally, notice that \(\cos(10 \pi t)\) has exactly 5 cycles on the interval \(0 \leq t \leq 1\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We will denote the frequency by \(\omega\text{,}\)

    Aside
    Frequency \(\omega\)
    🔗
    the lower case letter omega from the Greek alphabet. If
    \begin{equation*} \omega = \mbox{frequency} = \dfrac{b}{2\pi}, \end{equation*}
    then \(b = 2 \pi \omega\text{.}\) Therefore, the basic circular functions of frequency \(\omega\) are \(\cos(2 \pi \omega t)\) and \(\sin(2 \pi \omega t)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Suppose we take the basic sine function

    Aside
    Amplitude
    🔗
    \(\sin(2 \pi \omega t)\) of frequency \(\omega\) and multiply it by a factor \(A\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} y = A \sin(2 \pi \omega t). \end{equation*}
    The graph of this function oscillates between \(y = -A\) and \(y = +A\text{.}\) The number \(A\) is called the amplitude of the function.
    🔗

Both kinds of fluctuations can be described using circular functions of time \(t\text{.}\) The amplitude and the frequency of these functions have the physical interpretations given in the following table.

    Phase.

    🔗
    There is still another aspect of circular functions to consider besides amplitude and frequency. It is called phase difference. We can illustrate this with the two functions graphed below. They have the same amplitude and frequency, but differ in phase.
    🔗
    Specifically, the variable \(u\) in the expression \(\sin(u)\) is called the phase. In the dotted graph the phase is \(u = bt\text{,}\) while in the solid graph it is \(u = bt - \varphi\text{.}\) They differ in phase by \(bt - (bt - \varphi) = \varphi\text{.}\) In the exercises you will see why a phase difference of \(\varphi\) produces a shift—which we call a
    🔗
    phase shift—of \(\varphi/b\) in the graphs. (\(\varphi\) is the Greek letter phi.)
    🔗
