    Section 3.6 The Chain Rule

    Subsection 3.6.1 Combining Rates of Change

    Let’s return to the expanding house that we studied in section 4. When the temperature \(T\) increased, every side \(s\) of the house got longer; when \(s\) got longer, the volume \(V\) got larger. We already discussed how \(V\) responds to changes in \(s\text{,}\) but that’s only part of the story. What we’d really like to know is this: exactly how does the volume \(V\) respond to changes in temperature \(T\text{?}\) We can work this out in stages: first we see how \(V\) responds to changes in \(s\text{,}\) and then how \(s\) responds to changes in \(T\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Stage 1..

    Our “house” is a cube that measures 200 inches on a side, and the microscope equation (section 4) describes how \(V\) responds to changes in \(s\text{:}\)

    Aside
    How volume responds to changes in length
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx 120000 \ \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches of volume}}{\mbox{inch of length}} \cdot \Delta s \ \mbox{inches}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Stage 2..

    Physical experiments with wood show that a 200 inch length of wood increases about 0.0004 inches in length per degree Fahrenheit. This is a rate, and we can build a second microscope equation with it:

    Aside
    How length responds to changes in temperature
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta s \approx 0.0004\ \dfrac{\mbox{inches of length}}{\mbox{degree F}} \cdot \Delta T \ \mbox{degrees F}, \end{equation*}
    where \(\Delta T\) measures the change in temperature, in degrees Fahrenheit.
    🔗
    We can combine the two stages because \(\Delta s\) appears in both. Replace \(\Delta s\) in the first equation by the right-hand side of the second equation. The result is
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx 120000 \ \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{inch}} \times 0.0004\ \dfrac{\mbox{inches}}{\mbox{degree F}} \cdot \Delta T \ \mbox{degrees F}. \end{equation*}
    We can condense

    Aside
    How volume responds to changes in temperature
    🔗
    this to
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx 48 \ \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{degree F}} \cdot \Delta T \ \mbox{degrees F}. \end{equation*}
    This is a third microscope equation, and it shows directly how the volume of the house responds to changes in temperature. It is the answer to our question.
    🔗
    As always, the multiplier in a microscope equation is a rate. The multiplier in the third microscope equation, 48 cubic inches/degree F, tells us the rate at which volume changes with respect to temperature. Thus, if the temperature increases by 10 degrees between night and day, the house will become about 480 cubic inches larger. Recall that Bodanis (see section 4) said that the house might become only a few cubic inches larger—say, \(\Delta V = 3\) cubic inches. If we solve the microscope equation
    \begin{equation*} 3 \approx 48 \cdot \Delta T \end{equation*}
    for \(\Delta T\text{,}\) we see that the temperature would have risen only 1/16-th of a degree F!
    🔗
    The rate that appears as the multiplier in the third microscope equation is the product of the other two:

    Aside
    How the rates combine
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} 48 \ \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{degree F}} = 120000 \ \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{inch}} \times 0.0004 \ \dfrac{\mbox{inches}}{\mbox{degree F}}. \end{equation*}
    Each of these rates is a derivative:
    \begin{equation*} \underbrace {48 \ \rule[-15pt]{0pt}{15pt} \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{degree F}}}_ {\displaystyle dV/dT} = \underbrace {120000 \ \rule[-15pt]{0pt}{15pt} \dfrac{\mbox{cubic inches}}{\mbox{inch}}}_ {\displaystyle dV/ds} \times \underbrace {0.0004 \ \rule[-15pt]{0pt}{15pt} \dfrac{\mbox{inches}}{\mbox{degree F}}}_ {\displaystyle ds/dT}. \end{equation*}
    We wrote the derivatives in Leibniz’s notation because it’s particularly helpful in keeping straight what is going on. For instance, \(dV/dT\) indicates very clearly the rate at which volume is changing with respect to temperature, and \(dV/ds\) the rate at which it is changing with respect to length. These rates are quite different—they even have different units—but the notation \(V'\) does not distinguish between them. In Leibniz’s notation, the relation between the three rates takes this striking form:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dV}{dT} = \dfrac{dV}{ds} \cdot \dfrac{ds}{dT}. \end{equation*}
    This relation is called the chain rule for the variables \(T\text{,}\) \(s\text{,}\) and \(V\text{.}\) (We’ll see in a moment what this has to do with chains.)
    🔗
    The chain rule is a consequence of the way the three microscope equations are related to each other. We can see how it emerges directly from the microscope equations if we replace the numbers that appear as multipliers in those equations by the three derivatives. To begin, we write
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx \dfrac{dV}{ds} \cdot \Delta s \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \Delta s \approx \dfrac{ds}{dT} \cdot \Delta T. \end{equation*}
    Then, combining these equations, we get
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx \dfrac{dV}{ds} \cdot \dfrac{ds}{dT} \cdot \Delta T. \end{equation*}
    In fact, this is the microscope equation for \(V\) in terms of \(T\text{,}\) which can be written more directly as
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V \approx \dfrac{dV}{dT} \cdot \Delta T. \end{equation*}
    In these two expressions we have the same microscope equation, so the multipliers must be equal. Thus, we recover the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dV}{ds} \cdot \dfrac{ds}{dT} = \dfrac{dV}{dT}. \end{equation*}
    🔗

    Aside
    Recall that Leibniz worked directly with differentials, like \(dV\) and \(ds\text{,}\) so a derivative was a genuine fraction. For him, the chain rule is true simply because we can cancel the two appearances of “\(ds\)” in the derivatives. For us, though, a derivative is not really a fraction, so we need an argument like the one in the text to establish the rule.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.6.2 Chains and the Chain Rule

    Let’s analyze the relationships between the three variables in the expanding house problem in more detail. There are three functions involved: volume is a function of length: \(V = V(s)\text{;}\) length is a function of temperature: \(s = s(T)\text{;}\) and finally, volume is a function of temperature, too: \(V = V(s(T))\text{.}\) To visualize these relationships better, we introduce the notion of an input–output diagram. The input–output diagram for the function \(s = s(T)\) is just \(T \to s\text{.}\) It indicates that \(T\) is the input of a function whose output is \(s\text{.}\) Likewise \(s \to V\) says that volume \(V\) is a function of length \(s\text{.}\) Since the output of \(T \to s\) is the input of \(s \to V\text{,}\) we can make a chain of these two

    Aside
    An input–output chain
    🔗
    diagrams:
    \begin{equation*} T \longrightarrow s \longrightarrow V. \end{equation*}
    The result describes a function that has input \(T\) and output \(V\text{.}\) It is thus an input–output diagram for the third function \(V = V(s(T))\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We could also write the input–output diagram for the third function simply as \(T \to V\text{;}\) in other words,
    \begin{equation*} T \longrightarrow V \qquad \mbox{equals} \qquad T \longrightarrow s \longrightarrow V. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We say that \(T \to s \to V\) is a chain

    Aside
    A chain and its links
    🔗
    that is made up of the two links \(T \to s\) and \(s \to V\text{.}\) Since each input–output diagram represents a function, we can attach a derivative that describes the rate of change of the output with respect to the input:
    🔗
    Here is a single picture that shows all the relationships:
    🔗
    We can thus relate the derivative \(dV/dT\) of the whole chain to the derivatives \(dV/ds\) and \(ds/dT\) of the individual links by
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dV}{dT} = \dfrac{dV}{ds} \cdot \dfrac{ds}{dT}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The same argument holds for any chain of functions. If \(u\) is a function of \(x\text{,}\) and if \(y\) is some function of \(u\text{,}\) then a small change in \(x\) produces a small change in \(u\) and hence in \(y\text{.}\) The total multiplier for the chain is simply the product of the multipliers of the individual links:
    🔗

    Theorem 3.6.3. The chain rule.

    The chain rule states:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    Moreover, an obvious generalization extends this result to a chain containing more than two links.
    🔗

    A simple example.

    We can sometimes use the chain rule without giving it much thought. For instance, suppose a bookstore makes an average profit of $3 per book, and its sales are increasing at the rate of 40 books per month. At what rate is its monthly profit increasing, in dollars per month? Does it seem clear to you that the rate is $120 per month?
    🔗
    Let’s analyze the question in more detail. There are three variables here:
    🔗
    The two known rates are
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dp}{ds} = 3 \ \dfrac{\mbox{dollars}}{\mbox{book}} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{ds}{dt} = 40 \ \dfrac{\mbox{books}}{\mbox{month}}. \end{equation*}
    The rate we seek is \(dp/dt\text{,}\) and we find it by the chain rule:
    \begin{align*} dfrac{dp}{dt} \amp= \dfrac{dp}{ds} \cdot \dfrac{ds}{dt}\\ [3pt] \amp= 3 \ \dfrac{\mbox{dollars}}{\mbox{book}} \times 40 \ \dfrac{\mbox{books}}{\mbox{month}}\\ [3pt] \amp= 120 \ \dfrac{\mbox{dollars}}{\mbox{month}} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Chains, in general..

    The chain rule applies whenever the output of one function is the input of another. For example, suppose \(u = f(x)\) and \(y = g(u)\text{.}\) Then \(y = g(f(x))\text{,}\) and we have:
    🔗
    Let’s take
    \begin{equation*} u = x^2 \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad y = \sin(u); \end{equation*}
    then \(y = \sin(x^2)\text{,}\) and it is not at all obvious what the derivative \(dy/dx\) ought to be. None of the basic rules in section 4 covers this function. However, those rules do cover \(u = x^2\) and \(y = \sin(u)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{du}{dx} = 2x \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{dy}{du} = \cos(u). \end{equation*}
    We can now get \(dy/dx\) by the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx} = \cos(u) \cdot 2x. \end{equation*}
    Since we are interested in \(y\) as a function of \(x\)—rather than \(u\)—we should rewrite \(dy/dx\) so that it is expressed entirely in terms of \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{If} \quad y = \sin(x^2), \quad \mbox{then} \quad \dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2x \cos(u) = 2x \cos(x^2). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s start over, using the function names \(f\) and \(g\) we introduced at the outset:
    \begin{equation*} u = f(x) \quad \mbox{and} \quad y = g(u), \quad \mbox{so} \quad y = g(f(x)). \end{equation*}
    The third function, \(y = g(f(x))\text{,}\) needs a name of its own; let’s call it \(h\text{.}\) Thus

    Aside
    Composition of functions
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} y = h(x) = g(f(x)). \end{equation*}
    We say that \(h\) is composed of \(g\) and \(f\text{,}\) and \(h\) is called the , or the , of \(g\) and \(f\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The problem is to find the derivative \(h'\) of the composite function, knowing \(g'\) and \(f'\text{.}\) Let’s translate all the derivatives into Leibniz’s notation.
    \begin{equation*} h'(x) = \dfrac{dy}{dx} \qquad g'(u) = \dfrac{dy}{du} \qquad f'(x) = \dfrac{du}{dx}. \end{equation*}
    We can now invoke the chain rule:
    \begin{equation*} h'(x) = \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx} = g'(u) \cdot f'(x). \end{equation*}
    Although \(h'\) is now expressed in terms of \(g'\) and \(f'\text{,}\) we are not yet done. The variable \(u\) that appears in \(g'(u)\) is out of place—because \(h\) is a function of \(x\text{,}\) not \(u\text{.}\) (We got to the same point in the example; the original form of the derivative of \(\sin(x^2)\) was \(2x\cos(u)\text{.}\)) The remedy is to replace \(u\) by \(f(x)\text{;}\) we can do this because \(u = f(x)\) is given.
    🔗

    Observation 3.6.6. The chain rule.

    \begin{equation*} h'(x)=g'(f(x))\cdot f'(x) \text{ when } h(x)=g(f(x)) \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗
    There is a certain danger in a formula as terse and compact as this that it loses all conceptual meaning and becomes simply a formal string of symbols to be manipulated blindly. You should always remember that the expression in the box is just a mathematical statement of the intuitively clear idea that when two functions are chained together, with the output of one serving as the input of the other, then the combined function has a multiplier which is simply the product of the multipliers of the two constituent functions.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.6.3 Using the Chain Rule

    The chain rule will allow us to differentiate nearly any formula. The key is to recognize when a given formula can be written as a chain—and then, how to write it.
    🔗

    Example 3.6.8. Example 1.

    Here is a problem mentioned previously: What is the derivative of \(y = \sin(5x)\text{?}\) If we set
    \begin{equation*} y = \sin(u) \qquad \mbox{where} \qquad u = 5x, \end{equation*}
    then we find immediately
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{du} = \cos(u) \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{du}{dx} = 5. \end{equation*}
    Thus, by the chain rule we see
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{dy}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dx} = \cos(u) \cdot 5 = 5\cos(5x). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.6.9. Example 2.

    Suppose \(w = 2^{\cos{z}}\text{.}\) Set
    \begin{equation*} w = 2^u \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad u = \cos{z}. \end{equation*}
    Then, once again, the basic rules from section 4 are sufficient to differentiate the individual links:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dw}{du} = k_2 \, 2^u \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{du}{dz} = - \sin{z}. \end{equation*}
    The chain rule does the rest:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dw}{dz} = \dfrac{dw}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dz} = k_2 \, 2^u \cdot (-\sin{z}) = - k_2 \sin{z} 2^{\cos{z}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.6.10. Example 3.

    Suppose \(p = \sqrt{7t^3 + \sin^2{t}}\text{.}\) This presents several challenges. First let’s make a chain:
    \begin{equation*} p = \sqrt{u} \qquad \mbox{where} \qquad u = 7t^3 + \sin^2{t}. \end{equation*}
    The basic rules give us \(dp/du = 1/2\sqrt{u}\text{,}\) but it is more difficult to deal with \(u\text{.}\) Let’s at least introduce separate labels for the two terms in \(u\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} q = 7t^3 \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad r = \sin^2{t}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{du}{dt} = \dfrac{dq}{dt} + \dfrac{dr}{dt} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{dq}{dt} = 21 t^2. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The remaining term \(r = \sin^2{t} = (\sin{t})^2\) can itself be differentiated by the chain rule. Set
    \begin{equation*} r = v^2 \qquad \mbox{where} \qquad v = \sin{t}. \end{equation*}
    Then
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dr}{dv} = 2v \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \dfrac{dv}{dt} = \cos(t), \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dr}{dt} = \dfrac{dr}{dv} \cdot \dfrac{dv}{dt} = 2v \cos{t} = 2 \sin{t} \cos{t}. \end{equation*}
    The final step is to assemble all the pieces:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dp}{dt} = \dfrac{dp}{du} \cdot \dfrac{du}{dt} = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{u}} \cdot \left(21 t^2 + 2 \sin{t} \cos{t}\right) = \dfrac{21 t^2 + 2 \sin{t} \cos{t}} {2\sqrt{7t^3 + \sin^2{t}}} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By breaking down a complicated expression into simple pieces, and applying the appropriate differentiation rule to each piece, it is possible to differentiate a vast array of formulas. You may meet two sorts of difficulties: you may not see how to break down the expression into simpler parts; and you may overlook a step. Practice helps overcome the first, and vigilance the second.
    🔗
    🔗
    Here is an example of the second problem: find the derivative of \(y = -3 \cos(2x)\text{.}\) The derivative is not \(3 \sin(2x)\text{;}\) it is \(6\sin(2x)\text{.}\) Besides remembering to deal with the constant multiplier \(-3\text{,}\) and with the fact that there is a minus sign in the derivative of \(\cos{u}\text{,}\) you must not overlook the link \(u = 2x\) in the chain that connects \(y\) to \(x\text{.}\)
    🔗
    While we now have a way to find the derivative of a chain of two functions, we do not yet have a way to find the derivative of their product or quotient. These rules will be taken up in Chapter 5.
    🔗
    🔗
