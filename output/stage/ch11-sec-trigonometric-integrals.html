    Section 11.5 Trigonometric Integrals

    The preceding sections have covered the main integration techniques and concepts likely to be needed by most users of calculus. These techniques, together with the numerical methods discussed in chapter 11.6, should be part of the basic tool kit of every practitioner of calculus. For those going on in physics or mathematics, there are additional methods, largely involving trigonometric functions in various ways, that are sometimes useful. The purpose of this section is to develop the most commonly used of these techniques.
    🔗
    Recall that there are only a few simple antiderivatives we can write down immediately by inspection. All non-numerical integration techniques consist of finding transformations that will reduce some new class of integration problems to a class we already know how to solve. Once we have a new class of solvable problems, then we look for other classes of problems that can be reduced to this new class, and so on. The techniques we will be developing in this section involve ways of making such transformations through the use of basic trigonometric identities, typically in conjunction with integration by parts or by substitution. Before we proceed with the integration techniques, it will be helpful to list the trigonometric identities used.
    🔗

    Review of trigonometric identities.

    The most frequently used identity is
    \begin{equation*} \sin^2{x} + \cos^2{x} =1, \end{equation*}
    and the equivalent form obtained by dividing through by \(\cos^2{x}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \tan^2{x}+1=\sec^2{x}, \end{equation*}
    and by \(\sin^2{x}\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} 1+\cot^2{x}=\csc^2{x}. \end{equation*}
    The only other identities you will need have already been encountered:
    \begin{equation*} \sin{2x}=2\sin{x}\cos{x}\quad \mbox{and}\quad \cos{2x}=\cos^2{x}-\sin^2{x}, \end{equation*}
    plus the two other forms of the second of these identities,
    \begin{equation*} \cos^2{x}=\dfrac{1}{2}(1+\cos{2x})\quad \mbox{and}\quad \sin^2{x} =\dfrac{1}{2}(1-\cos{2x}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.5.1 Inverse Substitution

    🔗
    The method of substitution outlined in chapter 11.2 worked by taking a complicated integrand and breaking it down into simpler components, reducing the problem

    Aside
    Success sometimes comes by making things more complicated
    🔗
    of finding an antiderivative for something in the form \(f(G(x))g(x)\) to the problem of finding an antiderivative for \(f\text{.}\) In some cases, though, we go in the opposite direction: we have an integral \(\int f(x)\,dx\) we want to find but can’t evaluate directly. Instead, we can find a function \(G(u)\) with derivative \(g(u)\) such that we can find an antiderivative for \(f(G(u))g(u)\text{.}\) Since we know this integral is \(F(G(u))\text{,}\) we can now figure out what the desired function \(F\) must be. As with the earlier substitution techniques, this inverse substitution is conveniently expressed using differential notation.
    🔗
    [[EX:1]]Suppose we want to evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int \sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    If we substitute \(x=2\sin{u}\text{,}\) so that \(dx=2\cos{u}\,du\text{,}\) look what happens:
    \begin{align*} int \sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx \amp= \int \sqrt{4-(2\sin{u})^2}\,2\cos{u}\,du\\ \amp= \int \sqrt{4-4\sin^2{u}}\,2\cos{u}\,du\\ \amp= \int 2\sqrt{1-\sin^2{u}}\,2\cos{u}\,du\\ \amp= \int 2\cos{u}\,2\cos{u}\,du\\ \amp= 4\int \cos^2{u}\,du. \end{align*}
    But this is just an antiderivative we have already found in the exercises in chapter 11.3, namely
    \begin{align*} int \cos^2{u}\,du \amp= \dfrac{u}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4} \sin 2u + C\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{u}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4}\cdot 2\sin u \cos u+ C\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}(u +\sin u \cos u)+ C. \end{align*}
    To find the desired antiderivative for the original function of \(x\text{,}\) we now replace \(u\) by its expression in terms of \(x\) by inverting the relationship: If \(x=2\sin{u}\text{,}\) then \(\sin{u}=x/2\text{,}\) and \(u=\arcsin(x/2)\text{.}\) As we found in chapter 11.1, drawing a picture expressing the relationship between \(x\) and \(u\) makes it easy to visualize the other trigonometric functions:
    🔗
    From the picture we see that
    \begin{equation*} \cos{u} = \dfrac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \tan{u}=\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{4-x^2}}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can now find an expression for the desired antiderivative in terms of \(x\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} int \sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx \amp= 2(u +\sin u \cos u)+ C\\ \amp= 2\left(\arcsin\dfrac{x}{2} +\dfrac{x}{2}\dfrac{\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}\right) + C\\ \amp= 2\arcsin\dfrac{x}{2}+\dfrac{x\sqrt{4-x^2}}{2}+ C. \end{align*}
    As usual, you should check this result by differentiating the right-hand side to see that you do obtain the integrand on the left.
    🔗
    Similar substitutions

    Aside
    Some useful substitutions
    🔗
    allow us to evaluate other integrals involving square roots of quadratic expressions. Here is a summary of useful substitutions. In each case, \(a\) is a positive real number.
    🔗
    [[EX:2]]Integrate
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}. \end{equation*}
    If we set \(x=3\tan{u}\text{,}\) then \(dx=3\,\sec^2{u}\,du\text{,}\) and the integral becomes
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+9}} \amp= \dint \dfrac{3\,\sec^2{u}\,du}{\sqrt{9\,\sec^2{u}}}\\ \amp= \dint \sec{u}\,du\\ \amp= \ln{|\sec{u} + \tan{u}|}+C, \end{align*}
    (as we saw in chapter 11.4). To express this in terms of \(x\text{,}\) we again draw a picture showing the relation between \(u\) and \(x\text{:}\)
    🔗
    From this picture we see that \(\sec{u}=\sqrt{9+x^2}/3\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{x^2+9}} \amp= \ln\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{9+x^2}}{3} +\dfrac{x}{3}\right|+C\\ [3pt] \amp= \ln\left|\dfrac{\sqrt{9+x^2} +x}{3}\right|+C = \ln\left|\sqrt{9+x^2} +x \right|+C', \end{align*}
    where \(C'=C-\ln{3}\) is a new constant. As usual, you should differentiate to check that this really is the claimed antiderivative
    🔗
    [[EX:3]]Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{9x^2-16}}. \end{equation*}
    We first write \(\sqrt{9x^2-16}\) as \(\sqrt{9}\sqrt{x^2-(16/9)}=3\sqrt{x^2-(16/9)}\text{.}\) Using the substitution \(x=(4/3)\sec{u}\text{,}\) with \(dx=(4/3)\sec{u}\tan{u}\,du\) gives
    🔗
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{9x^2-16}} \amp= \dint \dfrac{(4/3)\sec{u}\,\tan{u}\,du}{3\sqrt{(16/9) \sec^2{u}-(16/9)}}\\ [6pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{(4/3)\sec{u}\,\tan{u}\,du}{3\cdot (4/3)\tan{u}} =\dfrac{1}{3}\dint \sec{u}\,du\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{3}\ln{| \sec{u}+\tan{u}|}+C. \end{align*}
    Again we need a picture to relate \(x\) and \(u\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Thus \(\tan{u}=\sqrt{9x^2-16}/4\text{,}\) which gives
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{9x^2-16}} \amp= \dfrac{1}{3}\ln{| \sec{u}+\tan{u}|}+C\\ \amp= \dfrac{1}{3}\ln\left|\dfrac{3x}{4} + \dfrac{\sqrt{9x^2-16}}{4}\right|+C\\ \amp= \dfrac{1}{3}\ln{\left|3x +\sqrt{9x^2-16}\right|} +C', \end{align*}
    where \(C'=C-(\ln{4})/3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As usual, you should differentiate this final expression to confirm that it really is the desired antiderivative.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.5.2 Inverse Substitution and Definite Integrals

    We saw on page  in chapter 11.2 how to use substitution to evaluate a definite integral. When we transformed an integral originally expressed in terms of a variable \(x\) into one expressed in terms of a variable \(u\text{,}\) the two integrals had the same numerical value. The same can be done with the inverse substitution technique we have just been considering. Let’s see how this works. Suppose we start with a function \(f(x)\) to be integrated over an interval \([a, b]\text{.}\) If we only knew an antiderivative \(F\) for \(f\text{,}\) we could easily write
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a), \end{equation*}
    as usual. In the examples we’ve just been considering, we found the antiderivative for \(f\) by making a substitution \(x=G(u)\) for some function \(G\) and then finding an antiderivative for \(f(G(u))g(u)\text{,}\) where \(G'=g\text{.}\) This antiderivative we know is \(F(G(u))\text{,}\) where \(F\) is the function we are trying to find. We were able to obtain \(F\) by replacing \(u\) by its expression in \(x\text{.}\) To do this we needed to find the inverse function \(G^{-1}\) for \(G\text{,}\) so that \(x=G(u)\) was equivalent to \(u=G^{-1}(x)\text{,}\) and \(F(x)=F(G(G^{-1}(x)))\text{.}\) It is this last step we can eliminate in calculating definite integrals.
    🔗
    If we want \(x\) to go from \(a\) to \(b\text{,}\) what must \(u\) do? What interval of \(u\) values will get transformed to this interval of \(x\) values by \(G\text{.}\) The value of \(u\) such that \(G(u)=a\) is \(A=G^{-1}(a)\text{.}\) Similarly the \(u\) value that gets transformed to \(b\) is \(B=G^{-1}(b)\text{.}\) Thus under the substitution \(x=G(u)\text{,}\) as \(u\) goes from \(A\) to \(B\text{,}\) \(x\) will go from \(a\) to \(b\text{.}\) Now look at the corresponding definite integral:
    \begin{align*} dint_A^B f(G(u))\,g(u)\,du \amp= \left.F(G(u))\rule{0pt}{16pt}\,\right|_A^B\\ \amp= F(G(B))-F(G(A))\\ \amp= F(G(G^{-1}(b)))-F(G(G^{-1}(a)))\\ \amp= F(b)-F(a), \end{align*}
    which is just the desired value of the original definite integral. To summarize,
    🔗
    Let’s look back at a couple of the preceding examples to see how this works.
    🔗
    [[EX:4]]Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-2}^2 \sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    In [[EX:1]] we found an antiderivative for this function by making the substitution \(x= 2\sin{u}=G(u)\text{.}\) The inverse function is then \(G^{-1}(x)=\arcsin(x/2)\text{.}\) To get \(x\) to go from \(-2\) to 2, \(u\) must go from \(G^{-1}(-2)=\arcsin(-1)=-\pi/2=A\) to \(G^{-1}(2)=\arcsin{1}=\pi/2=B\text{.}\) Then to evaluate the integral from \(x=-2\) to \(x=2\text{,}\) we only need to evaluate the antiderivative we found for the \(u\) integral between \(u=-\pi/2\) and \(u=\pi/2\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-2}^2 \sqrt{4-x^2}\,dx = \left.2(u+\sin{u}\,\cos{u})\rule{0pt}{16pt}\,\right|_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} = \pi-(-\pi)=2\pi. \end{equation*}
    (Note that this is just half the area of a circle of radius 2. How could we have foreseen this result from the form of the problem?)

    Subsection 11.5.3 Completing The Square

    Integrands involving terms of the form \(Ax^2+Bx+C\) can always be put in the form \(A( u^2\pm b^2)\) for a suitable variable \(u\) and constant \(b\text{.}\) The technique for doing this is the standard method of completing the square:
    \begin{align*} Ax^2+Bx+C \amp= A\left(x^2 + \dfrac{B}{A}x\right)+C\\ \amp= A\left(x^2 + \dfrac{B}{A}x + \dfrac{B^2}{4A^2}\right)+C - \dfrac{B^2}{4A}\\ \amp= A\left(x + \dfrac{B}{2A}\right)^{\!2} + \dfrac{4AC-B^2}{4A}. \end{align*}
    The substitutions
    \begin{equation*} u = x+ \dfrac{B}{2A} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad b = \dfrac{\sqrt{|4AC-B^2|}}{2A} \end{equation*}
    then transform the problem to a form where we can use the techniques already developed. The following examples should make this clear.
    🔗
    [[EX:6]]Consider the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{x^2+4x+5}. \end{equation*}
    This may not immediately remind us of anything we’ve seen before. But if we rewrite it in the form
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{(x^2+4x+4)+1}=\int \dfrac{dx}{(x+2)^2+1}, \end{equation*}
    it now begins to resemble something involving an arctangent. In fact, if we make the substitution \(u=x+2\text{,}\) so \(du=dx\text{,}\) we can write
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{x^2+4x+5} \amp= \dint \dfrac{du}{u^2+1}\\ \amp= \arctan{u}+C\\ \amp= \arctan(x+2) +C. \end{align*}
    🔗
    [[EX:7]]The technique of completing the square even works for expressions we could have factored directly, if we had noticed:
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{x^2+4x+3} \amp= \dint \dfrac{dx}{(x+2)^2-1}\\ [3pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{dx}{(x+2 -1)(x+2+1)}\\ [3pt] \amp= \dint \dfrac{dx}{(x+1)(x+3)}\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}\dint \dfrac{dx}{x+1} - \dfrac{1}{2}\dint \dfrac{dx}{x+3}\\ [5pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{2}\ln\left|\dfrac{x+1}{x+3}\right| +C. \end{align*}
    🔗
    [[EX:8]]Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{6x-x^2}}. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(6x-x^2=-(x^2-6x)=-(x-3)^2+9=9-(x-3)^2\text{.}\) If we now substitute \(x-3 =3u\text{,}\) with \(dx=3du\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{align*} dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{6x-x^2}} \amp= \dint \dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{9-(x-3)^2}}\\ \amp= \dint \dfrac{3 \, du}{\sqrt{9-9u^2}} = \dint \dfrac{du}{\sqrt{1-u^2}}\\ \amp= \arcsin{u}+C\\ \amp= \arcsin \dfrac{x-3}{3} +C. \end{align*}

    Subsection 11.5.4 Trigonometric Polynomials

    A trigonometric polynomial is any sum of constant multiples of products of trigonometric functions. The preceding techniques have shown some cases where such trigonometric polynomials can arise, even though the original problem had no apparent reference to trigonometric functions. There are many different ways of breaking trigonometric polynomials down into special cases which can then be integrated. We will develop one way which has the virtue of using few special cases, so that it can be used fairly automatically. It also introduces a powerful tool—that of reduction formula—which can be used to generate mathematical results interesting in their own right. One example is the striking representation of \(\pi\) derived in chapter 12.1. Other examples are developed in the exercises at the end of this section.
    🔗
    Since every trigonometric function is expressible in terms of sines and cosines, any trigonometric polynomial can be written as a sum of terms of the form \(c\,\sin^m{x}\,\cos^n{x}\) where \(c\) is a constant and \(m\) and \(n\) are integers—positive, negative, or 0. For instance, \(5\,\sec^2{x}\,\tan^5{x}\) can be rewritten as \(5\,\sin^5{x}\,\cos^{-7}{x}\text{.}\) To find antiderivatives for trigonometric polynomials, it therefore suffices to be able to evaluate integrals of the form
    \begin{equation*} \int \sin^m{x}\cos^n{x}\,dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We will see how to find antiderivatives for functions of this sort by breaking the problem into a series of special cases:
    🔗

    Category I: Either \(m\geq 0\) or \(n\geq 0\) (or both).

    Assume for the sake of explicitness that \(m\geq 0\text{.}\) We can then use the identity \(\sin^2{x}=1-\cos^2{x}\) to replace \(\sin^m{x}\) entirely by cosine terms if \(m\) is even, or to replace all but one of the sine terms by cosines if \(m\) is odd. A similar replacement can be made if \(n\geq 0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    [[EX:9]]
    \begin{align*} sin^4{x}\,\cos^6{x} \amp= (1-\cos^2{x})^2\cdot\cos^6{x}\\ \amp= (1-2\,\cos^2{x}+\cos^4{x})\cdot\cos^6{x}\\ \amp= \cos^6{x}-2\,\cos^8{x}+\cos^{10}{x}. \end{align*}
    (Note that in this example we could just as well have expressed \(\cos^6{x}\) entirely in terms of \(\sin{x}\text{.}\))
    🔗
    [[EX:10]]
    \begin{align*} sin^3{x}\,\cos^{-8}{x} \amp= \sin{x}\cdot (1-\cos^2{x})\cdot\cos^{-8}{x}\\ \amp= \sin{x}\,\cos^{-8}{x}-\sin{x}\,\cos^{-6}{x}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Category II: Both \(m<0\) and \(n<0\).

    If we divide the identity \(\cos^2{x}+\sin^2{x}=1\) by \(\sin^2{x}\,\cos^2{x}\text{,}\) we get the identity
    \begin{equation*} \sin^{-2}{x}+\cos^{-2}{x}=\sin^{-2}{x}\,\cos^{-2}{x}. \end{equation*}
    We will now use this identity to express anything of the form \(\cos^{-r}{x}\,\sin^{-s}{x}\) (where \(r>0\) and \(s>0\)) as a sum of terms of the form \(\sin^{-h}{x}\text{,}\) or \(\cos^{-i}{x}\text{,}\) or \(\sin{x}\,\cos^{-j}{x}\text{,}\) or \(\sin^{-k}{x}\,\cos{x}\text{.}\) Since we learned how to find antiderivatives for expressions like these in the previous cases, we will then be done.

    The trick in transforming \(cos^{-r}{x}\,\sin^{-s}{x}\) to the desired form is to multiply by \((\cos{x}\,\cos^{-1}{x})\) or \((\sin{x}\,\sin^{-1}{x})\) as needed so that both the sine and the cosine terms appear to even negative exponents.
