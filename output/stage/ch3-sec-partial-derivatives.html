    Section 3.7 Partial Derivatives

    Let’s return to the sunrise function once again. The time of sunrise depends not only on the date, but on our latitude. In fact, if we are far enough north or south, there are days when the sun never rises at all. We give in the table below the time of sunrise at eight different latitudes on March 15, 1990.
    🔗
    Thus on March 15, the time of sunrise increases as latitude increases.
    🔗
    Clearly what this shows

    Aside
    The time of sunrise depends on latitude as well as on the date
    🔗
    is that the time of sunrise is actually a function of two independent inputs: the date and the latitude. If \(T\) denotes the time of sunrise, then we will write \(T = T(d, \lambda)\) to make explicit the dependence of \(T\) on both the date \(d\) and the latitude \(\lambda\text{.}\) To capture this double dependence, we need information like the following table:
    🔗
    Thus we can say \(T(74, 42^\circ\mbox{N})\) = 6:13 (March 15 is the 74-th day of the year). Note, though, that at this date and place the time of sunrise is changing in two very different senses:
    🔗
    Two quite different rates are at work here, one with respect to time, the other with respect to latitude.
    🔗
    We need a notation which allows us to talk about the different rates at which a function can change,

    Aside
    A function of several variables has several rates of change
    🔗
    when that function depends on more than one variable. A rate of change is, of course, a derivative. But since a change in one input produces only part of the change that a function of several variables can experience, we call the rate of change with respect to any one of the inputs a partial derivative. If the value of \(z\) depends on the variables \(x\) and \(y\) according to the rule \(z = F(x,y)\text{,}\) then we denote the rate at which \(z\) is changing with respect to \(x\) when \(x = a\) and \(y = b\) by
    \begin{equation*} F_x(a,b) \qquad \mbox{or by} \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(a,b). \end{equation*}
    We call this rate the

    Aside
    Partial derivatives
    🔗
    🔗
    partial derivative of \(F\) with respect to \(x\). Similarly, we define the partial derivative of \(F\) with respect to \(y\) to be the rate at which \(z\) is changing when \(y\) is varied. It is denoted
    \begin{equation*} F_y(a,b) \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(a,b). \end{equation*}
    There is nothing conceptually new involved here; to calculate either of these partial derivatives you simply hold one variable constant and go through the same limiting process as before for the input variable of interest. Note that, to call attention to the fact that there is more than one input variable present, we write
    \begin{equation*} \frac{\partial z}{\partial x} \qquad \mbox{rather than} \qquad \frac{dz}{dx}, \end{equation*}
    as we did when \(x\) was the only input variable.
    🔗
    To calculate the partial derivative of \(F\) with respect to \(x\) at the point \((a,b)\text{,}\) we can use
    \begin{equation*} F_x(a,b) = \frac{\partial z}{\partial x}(a,b) = \lim_{\Delta x \rightarrow 0} \frac{F(a + \Delta x,b) - F(a,b)}{\Delta x}. \end{equation*}
    Similarly,
    \begin{equation*} F_y(a,b) = \frac{\partial z}{\partial y}(a,b) = \lim_{\Delta y \rightarrow 0} \frac{F(a,b+\Delta y) - F(a,b)}{\Delta y}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    By using this notation for partial derivatives, we can cast some of our earlier statements about the sunrise function \(T = T(d, \lambda)\) in the following form:
    \begin{align*} T_d(74, 42^\circ\mbox{N}) \amp\approx -1.63 \ \mbox{minutes per day};\\ T_\lambda(74,42^\circ\mbox{N}) \amp\approx +.25 \ \mbox{minutes per degree}. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Subsection 3.7.1 Partial Derivatives as Multipliers

    For any given date \(d\) and latitude \(\lambda\) we can write down two microscope equations for the sunrise function \(T(d, \lambda)\text{.}\) One describes how the time of sunrise responds to changes in the date, the other to changes in the latitude. Let’s consider variations in the time of sunrise in the vicinity of March 15 and \(42^\circ\)N.
    🔗
    The partial derivative \(T_d(74,42^\circ\mbox{N})\) of \(T\) with respect to \(d\) is the multiplier in the first of these microscope equations:

    Aside
    The microscope equation for dates
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta T \approx T_d(74,42^\circ\mbox{N}) \cdot \Delta d. \end{equation*}
    For example, from March 15 to March 17 (\(\Delta d\) = 2 days), we would expect the time of sunrise to change by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta T \approx -1.63 \ \frac{\mbox{min}}{\mbox{day}} \times 2 \ \mbox{days} = - 3.3 \ \mbox{minutes}. \end{equation*}
    Thus, we would expect the time of sunrise on March 17 at \(42^\circ\)N to be approximately
    \begin{equation*} T(76,42^\circ\mbox{N}) \approx \mbox{6:09.7} . \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The partial derivative \(T_{\lambda}(74,42^\circ\mbox{N})\) of \(T\) with respect to \(\lambda\) is the multiplier in the second microscope equation:

    Aside
    The microscope equation for latitudes
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta T \approx T_\lambda(74,42^\circ\mbox{N}) \cdot \Delta \lambda. \end{equation*}
    If, say, we moved \(1^\circ\) north, to \(43^\circ\)N, we would expect the time of sunrise on March 7 to change by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta T \approx 0.25 \ \frac{\mbox{min}}{\mbox{deg}} \times 1 \ \mbox{degree} = 0.25 \ \mbox{minute}. \end{equation*}
    The time of sunrise on March 15 at \(43^\circ\)N would therefore be
    \begin{equation*} T(74,43^\circ\mbox{N}) \approx \mbox{6:13.25}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We have seen what happens to the time of sunrise from March 15 to March 17 if we stay at \(42^\circ\)N, and we have seen what happens to the time on March 15 if we move from \(42^\circ\)N to \(43^\circ\)N. Can we put these two pieces of information together? That is, can we determine the time of sunrise on March 17 at \(43^\circ\)N? This involves changing both the date and the latitude.
    🔗
    To determine the total change we shall just

    Aside
    The total change
    🔗
    combine the two changes \(\Delta T\) we have already calculated. Making the date two days later moves the time of sunrise 3.3 minutes earlier, and travelling one degree north makes the time of sunrise 0.25 minutes later, so the net effect would be to change the time of sunrise by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta T \approx - 3.3 \ \mbox{min} + 0.25 \ \mbox{min} \approx - 3 \ \mbox{minutes}. \end{equation*}
    This puts the time of sunrise at \(T(76,43^\circ\mbox{N}) \approx\) 6:10.
    🔗
    We can formulate this idea more generally in the following way: partial derivatives are not only multipliers for gauging the separate effects that changes in each input have on the output, but they also serve as multipliers for gauging the cumulative effect that changes in all inputs have on the output. In general, if \(z=F(x,y)\) is a function of two variables, then near the point \((a,b)\text{,}\) the combined change in \(z\) caused by small changes in \(x\) and \(y\) can be stated by the full microscope equation:
    🔗

    Observation 3.7.3. The full microscope equation:.

    \begin{equation*} \Delta z \approx F_x(a,b) \cdot \Delta x + F_y(a,b) \cdot \Delta y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    As was the case for the functions of one variable, there is an important class of functions for which we may write “=” instead of “\(\approx\)” in this relation, the linear functions. The most general form of a linear function of two variables is \(z=F(x,y)= mx + ny + c\text{,}\) for constants \(m\text{,}\) \(n\text{,}\) and \(c\text{.}\)
    🔗
    In the exercises you will have an opportunity to verify that for a linear function \(z = F(x,y) = mx + ny + c\) and for all \((a,b)\text{,}\) we know that \(F_x(a,b) = m\) and \(F_y(a,b) = n\text{,}\) and the full microscope equation \(\Delta z = F_x(a,b) \cdot \Delta x + F_y(a,b) \cdot \Delta y\) is true for all values of \(\Delta x\) and \(\Delta y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 3.7.2 Formulas for Partial Derivatives

    No new rules are needed to find the formulas for the partial derivatives of a function of two variables that is given by a formula.

    Aside
    To find a partial derivative, treat the other variable as a constant
    🔗
    To find the partial derivative with respect to one of the variables, just treat the other variable as a constant and follow the rules for functions of a single variable. (The basic rules are described in section 5 and the chain rule in section 6.) We give two examples to illustrate the method.
    🔗

    Example 3.7.4. Example 1.

    For \(z = F(x,y) = 3x^2y + 5y^2 \sqrt{x}\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} F_x(x,y) \amp= 3y (2x) + 5y^2 \frac{1}{2 \sqrt{x}} = 6xy + \frac{5y^2}{2 \sqrt{x}}\\ F_y(x,y) \amp= 3x^2 + 10y \sqrt{x} \end{align*}
    🔗
    🔗

    Example 3.7.5. Example 2.

    For \(w = G(u,v) = 3 u^5 \sin{v} - \cos{v} + u\text{,}\) we have
    \begin{align*} \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial u} \amp= 15 u^4 \sin{v} + 1\\ \dfrac{\partial w}{\partial v} \amp= 3u^5 \cos{v} + \sin{v} \end{align*}
    🔗
    The formulas for derivatives and the combined multiplier effect of partial derivatives allow us to determine the effect of changes in length and in width on the area of a rectangle. The area \(A\) of the rectangle is a simple function of its dimensions \(l\) and \(w\text{,}\) \(A = F(l,w) = lw\text{.}\) The partial derivatives of the area are then
    \begin{equation*} F_l(l,w) = w \hspace{0.25in} \mbox{and} \hspace{0.25in} F_w(l,w) = l. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Changes \(\Delta l\) and \(\Delta w\) in the dimensions produce a change

    Aside
    The full microscope equation for rectangular area
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \Delta A \approx w \cdot \Delta l + l \cdot \Delta w \end{equation*}
    in the area. The picture below shows that the exact value of \(\Delta A\) includes an additional term—namely \(\Delta l \cdot \Delta w\)—that is not in the approximation \(w \cdot \Delta l + l \cdot \Delta w\text{.}\) The difference, \(\Delta l \cdot \Delta w\text{,}\) is very small when the changes \(\Delta l\) and \(\Delta w\) are small. In Chapter 5 we will have a further look at the nature of this approximation.
    🔗

    🔗
