    Section 11.2 Integration by Substitution

    In the preceding section we converted a couple of general rules for differentiation—the rule for the derivative of a constant times a function and the rule for the derivative of the sum of two functions—into equivalent rules in integral form. In this section we will develop the integral form of the chain rule and see some of the ways this can be used to find antiderivatives.
    🔗
    Suppose we have functions \(F\) and \(G\text{,}\) with corresponding derivatives \(f\) and \(g\text{.}\) Then the chain rule says
    \begin{equation*} \left(F(G(x))\right)'=F'(G(x))\,G'(x)=f(G(x))\,g(x). \end{equation*}
    If we now take the indefinite integral of these equations, we get
    \begin{equation*} F(G(x))+C=\int (F(G(x)))'\,dx=\int f(G(x))\,g(x)\,dx , \end{equation*}
    where \(C\) can be any constant.
    🔗
    Reversing these equalities

    Aside
    The integral form of the chain rule
    🔗
    to get a statement about integrals, we obtain:
    🔗
    This somewhat unpromising expression turns out to be surprisingly useful. Here’s how: Suppose we want to find an indefinite integral, and see in the integrand a pair of functions \(G\) and \(g\text{,}\) where \(G'=g\) and where \(g(x)\) can be factored out of the integrand.

    Aside
    Reduction methods transform problems into equivalent, simpler, problems
    🔗
    We then find a function \(f\) so that the integrand can be written in the form \(f(G(x))g(x)\text{.}\) Now we only have to find an antiderivative for \(f\text{.}\) Once we have such an antiderivative, call it \(F\text{,}\) then the solution to our original problem will be \(F(G(x))\text{.}\) Thus, while our original antiderivative problem is not yet solved, it has been reduced to a different, simpler antiderivative problem that, when all goes well, will be easier to evaluate. Such reduction methods are typical of many integration techniques. We will see other examples in the remainder of this chapter.
    🔗
    [[EX:1]]Suppose we try to find a formula for the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int 3x^2 \left(1 + x^3\right)^7 dx. \end{equation*}
    One way would be to multiply out the expression \(\left(1 + x^3\right)^7\text{,}\) making the integrand a polynomial with many separate terms of different degrees. (The highest degree would be 23; do you see why?) We could then carry out the integration “term-by-term,” using the rules for sums and constant multiples of powers of \(x\) that were given in the previous section. But this is tedious—even excruciating.
    🔗
    Instead, notice that the expression \(3x^2\) is the derivative of \(1+x^3\text{.}\) If we let \(G(x)=1+x^3\) and \(g(x)=3x^2\text{,}\) we can then write the integrand as
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2 \left(1 + x^3\right)^7=(G(x))^7g(x). \end{equation*}
    Now \((G(x))^7\) is clearly just \(f(G(x))\) where \(f\) is the function which raises its input to the 7th power—\(f(x)=x^7\) for any input \(x\text{.}\) But we recognize \(f\) as an elementary function whose antiderivative we can write down immediately as \(F(x)=\tfrac{1}{8}x^8\text{.}\) Thus the solution to our original problem will be
    \begin{equation*} \int 3x^2 \left(1 + x^3\right)^7 dx = F(G(x))+C = \dfrac{1}{8}\left(1 + x^3\right)^8+C. \end{equation*}
    As with any integration problem, we can check our answer by taking the derivative of the right-hand side to see if it agrees with the integrand on the left. You should do this whenever you aren’t quite sure of your technique (or your arithmetic!).
    🔗
    Note that the term \(3x^2\) that appeared in the integrand above was essential for the procedure to work. The integral
    \begin{equation*} \int \left(1 + x^3 \right)^7 dx \end{equation*}
    cannot be found by substitution, even though it appears to have a simpler form. (Of course, the integral can be found by multiplying out the integrand.)
    🔗
    Using differential notation] So far the symbol \(dx\) (the differential of \(x\)) under the integral sign has simply been an appendage, tagging along to suggest the \(\Delta x\) portion of the Riemann sums approximating definite integrals. It turns out we can take advantage of this notation to use the integral form of the chain rule more compactly.
    🔗
    Instead of naming the functions \(G\) and \(g\) as above, we introduce a new variable \(u=G(x)\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{du}{dx} = G'(x)=g(x), \end{equation*}
    and it is suggestive to multiply out this “quotient” to get
    \begin{equation*} du=g(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    While this is reminiscent of the microscope equation we met in chapter 3, and 18th century mathematicians took this equation seriously as a relation between two “infinitesimally small” quantities \(dx\) and \(du\text{,}\) we will view it only as a convenient mnemonic device. To see how this simplifies computations, reconsider the previous example. If we let \(u=1+x^3\text{,}\) then \(du=3x^2\,dx\text{,}\) so we can write
    \begin{equation*} 3x^2 (1 + x^3)^7 \, dx = (\underbrace{1 + \rule[-3pt]{0pt}{6pt}x^3}_ {\displaystyle u})^7 \underbrace{3x^2 \rule[-3pt]{0pt}{6pt}\, dx}_ {\displaystyle du} = u^7 \, du. \end{equation*}
    It follows that
    \begin{align*} int 3x^2 \left(1 + x^3 \right)^7 dx \amp= \int u^7 \, du\\ \amp= \tfrac{1}{8} u^8 + C\\ \amp= \tfrac{1}{8} \left(1 + x^3\right)^8 + C, \end{align*}
    as before. We have arrived at the same answer without having to introduce the cumbersome language of all the auxiliary functions—we simply substituted the variable \(u\) for a certain expression in \(x\) (which we called \(G(x)\) before), and replaced \(G'(x)dx\) by \(du\text{.}\) For this reason this technique is called integration by substitution. You should always be clear, though, that integration by substitution is just the integral form of the chain rule, a relationship that becomes clear whenever you check the answer substitution gives you.
    🔗
    [[EX:2]]Can we use the method of substitution to find
    \begin{equation*} \int \dfrac{e^{5x}}{6 + e^{5x}} \, dx? \end{equation*}
    The numerator is almost the derivative of the denominator. This suggests we let \(G(x) = 6+e^{5x}\text{,}\) giving \(g(x)=G'(x)= 5e^{5x}\text{.}\) Since we need to be able to factor \(g(x)\) out of the integrand, we multiply numerator and denominator by 5 to get
    \begin{align*} int \dfrac{e^{5x}}{6 + e^{5x}} \, dx \amp= \int \dfrac{1}{5} \cdot \dfrac{1}{6+e^{5x}}5e^{5x}\,dx\\ \amp= \int \dfrac{1}{5} \cdot \dfrac{1}{G(x)}g(x)\,dx\\ \amp= \dfrac{1}{5}\int f(G(x))g(x)\, dx, \end{align*}
    where \(f\) is just the reciprocal function—\(f(x)=1/x\text{.}\) But an antiderivative for \(f\) is just \(\ln{x}\text{,}\) so the desired antiderivative is just
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{5}F(G(x))+C= \dfrac{1}{5}\ln(6+e^{5x})+C. \end{equation*}
    As usual, you should check this answer by differentiating to see that you really do get the original function.
    🔗
    Now let’s see how this works using differential notation. If we set \(u = 6 + e^{5x}\text{,}\) then
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{du}{dx} = 5e^{5x} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad du = 5e^{5x} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Again we insert a factor of 5 in the numerator and an identical one in the denominator to balance it. Substitutions for \(u\) and \(du\) then yield the following:
    \begin{align*} int \dfrac{1}{5}\cdot \dfrac{5e^{5x}}{6 + e^{5x}} \, dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{5} \int \dfrac{5e^{5x} \, dx}{6 + e^{5x}}\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{5} \int \dfrac{du}{u}\\ [4pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{5} \ln(u) + C\\ [3pt] \amp= \dfrac{1}{5} \ln(6+e^{5x}) + C, \end{align*}
    🔗
    as before.
    🔗
    The two examples above have the same structure.

    Aside
    The basic structure
    🔗
    In both, a certain function of \(x\) is selected and called \(u\text{;}\) part of the integrand, namely \(u'\,dx\text{,}\) becomes \(du\text{,}\) the rest becomes one of the basic functions of \(u\text{.}\) Specifically:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{ccccccc} \mbox{integrand} && u && du && \mbox{function of $u$} \\[2pt] \hline 3x^2 \left(1 + x^3\right)^7 dx && 1 + x^3 && 3x^2 \rule{0pt}{18pt}\, dx && u^7 \\[4pt] \dfrac{e^{5x}}{6 + e^{5x}} \, dx && 6 + e^{5x} && 5e^{5x} \, dx && \dfrac{1}{5} \cdot \dfrac{1}{u} \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Note that you may have to do a bit of algebraic reshaping of the integrand to cast it in the proper form. For example, we had to insert a factor of 5 to the numerator of the second example to make the numerator be the derivative of the denominator. There is no set routine to be followed to find an antiderivative most efficiently, or even any way to know whether a particular method will work until you try it. Success comes with experience and a certain amount of intelligent fiddling until something works out.
    🔗
    [[EX:3]]The method of substitution is useful in simple problems, too. Consider
    \begin{equation*} \int \cos(3t) \, dt. \end{equation*}
    If we set \(u = 3t\text{,}\) then \(du = 3 \, dt\) and
    \begin{align*} int \cos(3t) \, dt \amp= \int \cos(u) \cdot \tfrac{1}{3} \, du\\ [2pt] \amp= \tfrac{1}{3} \int \cos(u) \, du\\ [4pt] \amp= \tfrac{1}{3} \sin(u) + C\\ [6pt] \amp= \tfrac{1}{3} \sin(3t) + C. \end{align*}
    🔗

    Subsection 11.2.1 Substitution in Definite Integrals

    Until now we have been using the technique of substitution to find antiderivatives—that is, to evaluate indefinite integrals. Refer back to the integral form of the chain rule given in the box on page , and see what happens when we use this equation to evaluate a definite integral. Suppose we want to evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^bf(G(x))\,g(x)\,dx. \end{equation*}
    We know that \(F(G(x))\) is an antiderivative, so by the fundamental theorem we have
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^bf(G(x))\,g(x)\,dx=\left. F(G(x))\right|_a^b=F(G(b))-F(G(a)). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now suppose we make the substitution \(u=G(x)\) and \(du=g(x)\,dx\text{.}\) Then as \(x\) goes from \(a\) to \(b\text{,}\) \(u\) will go from \(G(a)\) to \(G(b)\text{.}\) Moreover, we have
    \begin{equation*} \int_{G(a)}^{G(b)} f(u)\,du=\left. F(u)\right|_{G(a)}^{G(b)} = F(G(b))-F(G(a)), \end{equation*}
    so the two definite integrals have the same value. In other words,
    🔗
    This means that to evaluate a definite integral by substitution, we can do everything in terms of \(u\text{.}\) We don’t ever need to find an antiderivative for the original integrand in terms of \(x\) or use the original limits of integration.
    🔗
    [[EX:4]]Consider the definite integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \dfrac{\cos{x}\,dx}{1+\sin{x}}. \end{equation*}
    We can evaluate this integral by making the substitution \(u=1+\sin{x}\text{,}\) and \(du=\cos{x}\,dx\text{.}\) Moreover, as \(x\) goes from 0 to \(\pi/2\text{,}\) \(u\) goes from 1 to 2. Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\pi/2} \dfrac{\cos{x}\,dx}{1+\sin{x}} = \int_1^2 \dfrac{du}{u}=\left.\ln{u}\right|_1^2=\ln{2}. \end{equation*}
    Check that this is the same answer you would have gotten if you had expressed the antiderivative \(\ln{u}\) in terms of \(x\) and evaluated the result at the limits on the original integral.
    🔗
    [[EX:5]]Evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 6 x^2(1+x^3)^4\,dx. \end{equation*}
    With the substitution \(u=1+x^3\) and \(du=3x^2\,dx\text{,}\) as \(x\) goes from 0 to 1, \(u\) goes from 1 to 2. Our integral thus becomes
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 6 x^2(1+x^3)^4\,dx=\int_0^2 2 u^4\,du =\left. \dfrac{2}{5}u^5 \, \right|_1^2 =\dfrac{64}{5}. \end{equation*}
