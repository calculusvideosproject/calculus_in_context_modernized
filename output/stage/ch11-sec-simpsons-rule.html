    Section 11.6 Simpson’s Rule

    This chapter has concentrated on formulas for antiderivatives,

    Aside
    A return to numerical methods
    🔗
    because a formula conveys compactly a lot of information. However, you must not lose sight of the fact that most antiderivatives cannot be found by such analytic methods. The integrand may be a data function, for instance, and thus have no formula. And even when the integrand is given by a formula, there may be no formula for the antiderivative itself. One possibility in such cases is to approximate such a function by a function—such as a polynomial—for which we can readily find an antiderivative. In chapter 10 we saw some methods for doing this. In chapter 12 we introduce Fourier series, providing another family of approximating functions for which antiderivatives can be readily obtained. Another approach is to find a desired definite integral using approximating rectangles, as we did in chapter 6.
    🔗
    In any case, numerical methods are inescapable, but accurate results require many calculations. This takes time—even on a modern high-speed computer. A numerical method is said to be efficient if it gets accurate results quickly, that is, with relatively few calculations. In chapter 6 we saw that midpoint Riemann sums are much more efficient than left or right endpoint Riemann sums.

    Aside
    Efficient numerical integration
    🔗
    We will look at these and other methods in detail in this section. The most efficient method we will develop is called Simpson’s rule.
    🔗

    Subsection 11.6.1 The Trapezoid Rule

    We interpret the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    as the area under the graph \(y = f(x)\) between \(x = a\) and \(x = b\text{.}\) We interpret a Riemann sum as the total area of a collection of rectangles that approximate the area under the graph.

    Aside
    Replace rectangles by trapezoids
    🔗
    The tops of the rectangles are level, and they represent the graph of step function. Clearly, we get a better approximation to the graph by using slanted lines. They form the tops of a sequence of trapezoids that approximate the area under the graph.
    🔗
    Let’s figure out the areas of these trapezoids.

    Aside
    Each trapezoid is sandwiched between two rectangles…
    🔗
    They are related in a simple way to the rectangles that we would construct at the right and left endpoints to calculate Riemann sums. To see the relation, let’s take a closer look at a single single trapezoid.
    🔗
    It is sandwiched between two rectangles, one taller and one shorter.

    Aside
    …whose average area equals the area of the trapezoid
    🔗
    In our picture the height of the taller rectangle is \(f(\mbox{right endpoint})\text{.}\) We will call it the right rectangle. The height of the shorter rectangle is \(f(\mbox{left endpoint})\text{.}\) We will call it the left rectangle. For other trapezoids the left rectangle may be the taller one. In any case, the trapezoid is exactly half-way between the two rectangles in size, and thus its area is the average of the areas of the rectangles:
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{area trapezoid} = \tfrac{1}{2} \left( \mbox{area left rectangle} + \mbox{area right rectangle} \right). \end{equation*}
    If we sum over the areas of all of the trapezoids,

    Aside
    The trapezoidal approximation is the average of left and right Riemann sums
    🔗
    the areas of the right rectangles sum to the right Riemann sum, and similarly for the left rectangles. In follows that
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{trapezoidal approximation} = \tfrac{1}{2}( \mbox{left Riemann sum} + \mbox{right Riemann sum}). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The pictures make it clear that the trapezoidal approximation should be significantly better than either a left or a right Riemann sum. To test this numerically, let’s get numerical estimates for the integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 \dfrac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln{3} = 1.098612288668\ldots . \end{equation*}
    (The relation between the trapezoid approximation and the left and right Riemann sums holds for any choice \(\Delta x_k\) of subintervals.

    Aside
    Comparing approximations
    🔗
    However, we will use equal subintervals to make the calculations simpler.) Here is how our four main estimates compare when we use 100 subintervals.
    🔗
    The figures in this table are calculated to 8 decimal places, and the column marked error is the difference
    \begin{equation*} 1.09861229 - \mbox{approximation}, \end{equation*}
    so that the error is negative if the approximation is too large. The left Riemann sum is too large, for instance.
    🔗
    Before we comment on the differences between the estimates, let’s gather more data. Here are the calculations for 1000 subintervals. Note that the midpoint and trapezoidal approximations are more than 1000 times better than the left or right Riemann sums!
    🔗
    We expected the trapezoidal approximation

    Aside
    A surprise: the midpoint approximation is even better than the trapezoidal
    🔗
    to be better than either the right or left Riemann sum. The surprising observation is that the midpoint Riemann sum is even better! In fact, it appears that the midpoint Riemann sum has only half the error of the trapezoidal approximation.
    🔗
    The figures below explain geometrically why the midpoint approximation is better than the trapezoidal. The first step is shown on the left. Take a midpoint rectangle (whose height is \(f(\mbox{midpoint})\)), and rotate the top edge around the midpoint until it is tangent to the graph of \(y = f(x)\text{.}\) Call this a midpoint trapezoid. Notice that the trapezoid has the same area as the rectangle.
    🔗
    The second step is to compare

    Aside
    Shading depicts the errors
    🔗
    the midpoint trapezoid to the one used in the trapezoidal approximation. This is done on the right. The error coming from the midpoint trapezoid is shaded light gray, while the error from the trapezoidal approximation is dark gray. The midpoint trapezoid is the better approximation. Since the midpoint rectangle has the same area as the midpoint trapezoid, we now see why the midpoint Riemann sum is more accurate than the trapezoidal approximation. This picture also explains why the errors of the two approximations have different signs, which we noticed first in the tables.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.6.2 Simpson’s Rule

    Our goal is a calculation scheme for integrals

    Aside
    Combine good approximations…
    🔗
    that gives an error is as small as possible. The trapezoidal and the midpoint approximations are both good—but we can combine them to get something even better. Here is why. The tables and the figure above indicate that the errors in the two approximations have opposite signs, and the midpoint error is only about half the size of the trapezoidal error (in absolute value). Thus, if we form the sum
    \begin{equation*} 2 \times \mbox{midpoint approximation} + \mbox{trapezoidal approximation}, \end{equation*}
    then most of the error will cancel.

    Aside
    …so that most of the error cancels
    🔗
    Now this sum is approximately three times the value of the integral, because each term in it approximates the integral itself. Therefore, if we divide by three, then
    \begin{equation*} \tfrac{2}{3} \times \mbox{midpoint approximation} + \tfrac{1}{3} \times \mbox{trapezoidal approximation} \end{equation*}
    should be a superb approximation to the integral.
    🔗
    Let’s try this approximation on our test integral
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 \dfrac{1}{x}\,dx \end{equation*}
    with \(n = 100\) subintervals. Using the numbers from the table on page , we obtain
    \begin{equation*} \tfrac{2}{3} \times 1.09859747 + \tfrac{1}{3} \times 1.09864191 = 1.098612283, \end{equation*}
    which gives the value of the integral accurate to 8 decimal places. This method of approximating integrals is called Simpson’s rule.
    🔗
    We can use the program RIEMANN to do the calculation.

    Aside
    Using Riemann sums to carry out Simpson’s rule
    🔗
    First calculate left and right Riemann sums, and take their average. That is the trapezoidal approximation. Then calculate the midpoint Riemann sum. Since
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{trapezoid} = \tfrac{1}{2} \times \mbox{left} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times \mbox{right}, \end{equation*}
    Simpson’s rule reduces to this combination of left, right, and midpoint sums:
    \begin{align*} lefteqn{\tfrac{2}{3} \times \mbox{midpoint} + \tfrac{1}{3} \times \mbox{trapezoidal}} \qquad\quad\\ \amp= \tfrac{2}{3} \mbox{midpoint} + \tfrac{1}{3} \left( \tfrac{1}{2} \times \mbox{left} + \tfrac{1}{2} \times \mbox{right} \right)\\ [3pt] \amp= \tfrac{2}{3} \times \mbox{midpoint} + \tfrac{1}{6} \times \mbox{left} + \tfrac{1}{6} \times \mbox{right}\\ [3pt] \amp= \tfrac{1}{6} \left( 4 \times \mbox{midpoint} + \mbox{left} + \mbox{right} \vphantom{\tfrac{1}{6}}\right) \end{align*}
    🔗
    You can get even more accuracy

    Aside
    The accuracy of Simpson’s rule
    🔗
    if you keep track of more digits in the left, right, and midpoint Riemann sums. For example, if you estimate
    \begin{equation*} \int_1^3 \dfrac{1}{x}\,dx = \ln{3} = 1.098\,612\,288\,668\ldots \end{equation*}
    to 14 decimal places, you will get the following.
    🔗
    When combined these give the estimate 1.098 612 288 997 3, which differs from the true value by less than \(3.3 \times 10^{-10}\text{.}\) In other words, the calculation is actually correct to 9 decimal places.
    🔗
    It is possible to get a bound on the error

    Aside
    An error bound for Simpson’s rule
    🔗
    produced by using Simpson’s rule to estimate the value of
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    (See the discussion of error bounds in chapter 6.3.) Specifically,
    \begin{equation*} \left| \, \int_a^b f(x) \, dx - \mbox{Simpson's rule } \right| \leq \dfrac{M (b - a)^5}{2880 \, n^4}, \end{equation*}
    where \(n\) is the number of subintervals used in the Riemann sums and \(M\) is a bound on the size of the fourth derivative of \(f\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} |f^{(4)}(x)| \leq M \qquad \mbox{for all $a \leq x \leq b$.} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The most important factor in the error bound

    Aside
    The crucial factor \(n^{-4}\)
    🔗
    is the \(n^4\) that appears in the denominator. In our example \(n = 100 = 10^2\text{,}\) and this leads to the factor \(1/n^4 = 10^{-8}\) in the error bound. As we saw, the actual error was less than \(10^{-9}\text{.}\) Essentially, \(n\) is the number of computations we do, and error bound tells how many decimal places of accuracy we can count on. According to the error bound, a ten-fold increase in the number of computations produces four more decimal places of accuracy. That is why Simpson’s method is efficient.
    🔗
    🔗

    Exercises 11.6.3 Exercises

    Because Simpson’s rule is so efficient, we can use it to get accurate values of some of the fundamental constants of mathematics. For example, since
    \begin{equation*} 4 \cdot \int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{1 + x^2} = \left. 4\arctan(x) \ \vphantom{\dfrac{1}{2}} \right|_0^1 = 4\arctan(1) = 4 \cdot \dfrac{\pi}{4} = \pi, \end{equation*}
    we can estimate the value of \(\pi\) by using Simpson’s rule to approximate this integral.
    🔗

    1.

    Evaluate the expression above (including the factor of 4) use Simpson’s rule with \(n = 2\text{,}\) 4, 8, and 16. How accurate is each of these estimates of \(\pi\text{;}\) that is, how many decimal places of each estimate agree with the true value of \(\pi\text{?}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    2.

    Over the interval \(0 \leq x \leq 1\) it is true that
    \begin{equation*} |f^{(4)}(x)| \leq 96 \qquad \mbox{when} \qquad f(x) = \dfrac{4}{1+x^2}. \end{equation*}
    (You don’t need to show this, but how might you do it?) Use this bound to show that \(n = 256 = 2^8\) will guarantee that you can find the first 10 decimals of \(\pi\) by using the method of the previous question.
    🔗

    (a)

    Show that if \(n = 128 = 2^7\) then the error bound for Simpson’s rule does not guarantee that you can find the first 10 decimals of \(\pi\) by the same method.
    🔗
