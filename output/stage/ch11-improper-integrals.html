    Section 11.7 Improper Integrals

    🔗

    Subsection 11.7.1 The Lifetime of Light Bulbs

    🔗
    Ordinary light bulbs are supposed to burn about 700 hours,

    Aside
    The lifetime of a light bulb is unpredictable
    🔗
    but of course some last longer while others burn out more quickly. It is impossible to know, in advance, the lifetime of a particular bulb you might buy, but it is possible to describe what happens to a large batch of bulbs.
    🔗
    Suppose we take a batch of 1000 light bulbs, start them burning at the same time, and note how long it takes each one to burn out. Let
    \begin{equation*} L(t) = \mbox{fraction of bulbs that burn out before $t$ hours} \end{equation*}
    Then \(L(t)\) might have a graph that looks like this:
    🔗
    In this example, \(L(400) \approx .5\text{,}\) so about half the bulbs burned out before 400 hours. Furthermore, all but a few have burned out by 1250 hours.
    🔗
    Manufacturers are very concerned

    Aside
    The burnout rate
    🔗
    about the way the lifetime of light bulbs varies. They study the output of their factories on a regular basis. It is more common, though, for them to talk about the rate \(r\) at which bulbs burn out. The rate varies over time, too. In fact, in terms of \(L\text{,}\) \(r\) is just the derivative
    \begin{equation*} r(t) = L'(t) \quad \mbox{bulbs per hour.} \end{equation*}
    However, if we start with the rate \(r\text{,}\) then we get \(L\) as the integral
    \begin{equation*} L(t) = \int_0^t r(s) \, ds. \end{equation*}
    This is yet another consequence of

    Aside
    Lifetime is the integral of burnout rate…
    🔗
    the fundamental theorem of calculus. The integral expression is quite handy. For example, the fraction of bulbs that burn out between \(t= a\) hours and \(t = b\) hours is
    \begin{equation*} L(b) - L(a) = \int_a^b r(s) \, ds. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    We can even use the integral to say that all the bulbs burn out eventually:
    \begin{equation*} L(t) = \int_0^t r(s) \, ds = 1 \qquad \mbox{when $t$ is sufficiently large.} \end{equation*}
    In practice \(r\) is the average burnout rate

    Aside
    …but there is no upper limit to the lifetime
    🔗
    for many batches of light bulbs, so we can’t identify the precise moment when \(L\) becomes 1. All we can really say is
    \begin{equation*} L(\infty) = \int_0^{\infty} r(s) \, ds. \end{equation*}
    This is called an improper integral,

    Aside
    An integral is improper if the domain of integration is infinite
    🔗
    because it cannot be calculated directly: its “domain of integration” is infinite. By definition, its value is obtained as a limit of ordinary integrals:
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\infty} r(s) \, ds = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b r(s) \, ds. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The normal density function of probability theory provides us with another example of an improper integral. In a simple form, the function itself is
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \exp\left(-\dfrac{x^2}{2}\right). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (Recall, \(\exp(x) = e^x\text{.}\)) If \(x\) is any normally distributed quantity whose average value is 0, then the probability that a randomly chosen value of \(x\) lies between the numbers \(a\) and \(b\) is
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    Since the probability that \(x\) lies somewhere

    Aside
    The normal probability distribution involves an improper integral
    🔗
    on the \(x\)-axis is 1, we have
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx = 1. \end{equation*}
    This is an improper integral, and its value is defined by the limit
    \begin{equation*} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} f(x) \, dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_{-b}^b f(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    In the exercises you will have a chance to evaluate this integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 11.7.2 Evaluating Improper Integrals

    🔗
    An integral with an infinite domain of integration is

    Aside
    An integral is also improper if its integrand becomes infinite
    🔗
    only one kind of improper integral. A second kind has a finite domain of integration, but the integrand becomes infinite on that domain. For example,
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{x} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \int_0^1 \ln{x} \, dx \end{equation*}
    are both improper in this sense. In both cases, the integrand becomes infinite as \(x \to 0\text{.}\) Because the difficulty lies at the endpoint 0, we define
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{x} = \lim_{a \to 0} \int_a^1 \dfrac{dx}{x}. \end{equation*}
    More generally,
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx \end{equation*}
    is an improper integral if \(f(x)\) becomes infinite at some point \(c\) in the interval \([a, b]\text{.}\) In that case we define
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^b f(x) \, dx = \lim_{q \to 0} \left(\int_a^{c-q} f(x) \, dx + \int_{c+q}^b f(x) \, dx\right). \end{equation*}
    In effect, we avoid the bad spot but “creep up” on it in the limit.
    🔗
    Indefinite integrals—that is,

    Aside
    Antiderivatives help find improper integrals
    🔗
    antiderivatives—can be a great help in evaluating improper integrals. Here are some examples.
    🔗

    [[EX:1]]..

    We can evaluate
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x} \, dx \end{equation*}
    by noting first that \(\dint e^{-x} \, dx = - e^{-x}\text{.}\) Therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^b e^{-x} \, dx = \left. - e^{-x} \right|_0^b = -e^{-b} - \left(-e^{-0}\right) = 1 - e^{-b} \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^{\infty} e^{-x} \, dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \int_0^b e^{-x} \, dx = \lim_{b \to \infty} \left(1-e^{-b}\right) = 1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    🔗

    [[EX:2]]..

    To evaluate \(\dint_0^1 \ln{x} \, dx\text{,}\) we use the indefinite integral
    \begin{equation*} \int \ln{x} \, dx = x\, \ln{x} - x. \end{equation*}
    Thus
    \begin{equation*} \int_a^1 \ln{x} \, dx = \left. x\,\ln{x} - x \rule{0pt}{14pt}\,\right|_a^1 = -1 - \left( a\ln{a} - a \right) = a-1 - a\ln{a}. \end{equation*}
    By direct calculation (using a graphing package, for instance) we can find
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{a \to 0} \ a\ln{a} = 0; \end{equation*}
    therefore
    \begin{equation*} \int_0^1 \ln{x} \, dx = \lim_{a \to 0} \int_a^1 \ln{x} \, dx = \lim_{a \to 0} \ a-1 - a\ln{a} = -1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    You should not assume that an improper integral always has a finite value, though. Consider the next example.
    🔗
    [[EX:3]]. \(\displaystyle \left. \dint_0^1 \dfrac{dx}{x} = \lim_{a \to 0} \int_a^1 \dfrac{dx}{x} = \lim_{a \to 0} \ln(x) \,\right|_a^1 = \lim_{a \to 0} \left( \ln(1)-\ln(a) \right) = \infty.\)
    🔗
    This is forced because \({\displaystyle \lim_{a \to 0} \ \ln(a) = -\infty}\text{,}\) which you can see from the graph of the logarithm function.
    🔗
    🔗
    🔗

    The normal density function.

    The next two questions concern the improper integral
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx \end{equation*}
    of the normal density function defined on page . The goal is to determine the value of this integral.
    🔗

    3.

    First, use RIEMANN to estimate the value of
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-b}^{b} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx \end{equation*}
    when \(b\) has the different values 1, 10, 100, and 1000. On the basis of these results, estimate
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{b \to \infty} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-b}^{b} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    This gives one estimate of the value of the improper integral.
    🔗
    🔗

    4.

    To construct a second estimate, begin by sketching the graph of the normal density function
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} e^{-x^2/2} \end{equation*}
    on an interval centered at the origin. Use the graph to argue that
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-b}^{b} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx = 2 \left( \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{0}^{b} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx \right) = \sqrt{2/\pi} \int_{0}^{b} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx \end{equation*}
    and therefore
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx = \sqrt{2/\pi} \int_{0}^{\infty} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    (a)
    Now consider the accumulation function
    \begin{equation*} F(t) = \sqrt{2/\pi} \int_{0}^{t} e^{-x^2/2} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    We want to find \(F(\infty) = {\displaystyle \lim_{t \to \infty} F(t)}\text{.}\) According to the fundamental theorem of calculus, \(y = F(t)\) satisfies the initial value problem
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = \sqrt{2/\pi} \cdot e^{-t^2/2}, \qquad y(0) = 0. \end{equation*}
    Use a differential equation solver (e.g., PLOT) to graph the solution \(y = F(t)\) to this problem. From the graph determine
    \begin{equation*} F(\infty) = \lim_{t \to \infty} F(t). \end{equation*}
    🔗

    The gamma function.

    🔗
    The factorial function is defined for a positive integer \(n\) by the formula
    \begin{equation*} n! = n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdot \cdots \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1. \end{equation*}
    For example, \(1! = 1\text{,}\) \(2! = 2\text{,}\) \(3! = 6\text{,}\) \(4! = 24\text{,}\) and \(10! = 3628800\text{.}\) The factorial function is used often in diverse mathematical contexts, but its use is sometimes limited by the fact that it is defined only for positive integers. How might the function be defined on an expanded domain, so that we could deal with expressions like \(tfrac{1}{2}!\text{,}\) for example? The gamma function answers this question.
    🔗
    The gamma function \(\Gamma(x)\) is defined by the improper integral
    \begin{equation*} \Gamma(x) = \int_0^{\infty} e^{-t} t^{x - 1} \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Notice that \(t\) is the active variable in this integral. While the integration is being performed, \(x\) is treated as a constant; for the integral to converge, we need \(x > 0\text{.}\)
    🔗

    5.

    Show that \(\Gamma(1) = 1\text{.}\)
    🔗

    6.

    Using integration by parts, show that \(\Gamma(x + 1) = x \cdot \Gamma(x)\text{.}\) You may use the fact that
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{t^p}{e^t} \to 0 \ \mbox{as} \ t \to +\infty. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The property \(\Gamma(x + 1) = x \cdot \Gamma(x)\) makes the gamma function like the factorial function, because
    \begin{equation*} (n+1)! = (n+1) \cdot \underbrace{n \cdot (n-1) \cdot (n-2) \cdot \cdots \cdot 3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1}_ {\displaystyle = n!} = (n+1) \cdot n!. \end{equation*}
    Notice there is a slight difference, though. We explore this now.
