    Section 11.3 Integration by Parts

    🔗
    As in the previous section,

    Aside
    An integral form of the product rule
    🔗
    suppose we have functions \(F\) and \(G\text{,}\) with corresponding derivatives \(f\) and \(g\text{.}\) If we use the product rule to differentiate \(F(x) \cdot G(x)\text{,}\) we get:
    \begin{equation*} \left( F \cdot G \right)' = F \cdot G' + F' \cdot G = F\cdot g + f\cdot G. \end{equation*}
    We can turn this into a statement about indefinite integrals:
    \begin{equation*} \int \left( F \cdot g + f \cdot G \right) \, dx = \int \left( F \cdot G \right)' \, dx = F(x) \cdot G(x) + C. \end{equation*}
    Unfortunately, in this form the statement is not especially useful; it applies only when the integrand has two terms of the special form \(f \cdot g' + f' \cdot g\text{.}\) However, if we rewrite the statement in the form
    🔗
    it becomes very useful.
    🔗
    [[EX:1]]We will use the formula in the box to find
    \begin{equation*} \int x \cdot \cos{x} \, dx. \end{equation*}
    If we label the parts of this integrand as follows:
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = x \qquad \qquad g(x) = \cos{x}, \end{equation*}
    then we have
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = 1 \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad G(x) = \sin{x}. \end{equation*}
    According to the formula,
    \begin{align*} int x \cdot \cos{x} \, dx \amp= x \cdot \sin{x} - \int \sin{x} \, dx\\ \amp= x \cdot \sin{x} + \cos{x} + C. \end{align*}
    🔗
    The integrand is first broken into two parts—in this case,

    Aside
    Integrate only part of the integrand
    🔗
    \(x\) and \(\cos{x}\text{.}\) One part is differentiated while the other part is integrated. (The part we integrated is \(g(x) = \cos{x}\text{,}\) and we got \(G(x) = \sin{x}\text{.}\)) For this reason, the rule described in the box is called integration by parts.
    🔗
    As with integration by substitution, integration by parts exchanges one integration task for another: Instead of finding an antiderivative for \(F \cdot g\text{,}\) we must find one for \(f \cdot G\text{.}\) The idea is to “trade-in” one integration problem for a more readily solvable one.
    🔗
    [[EX:2]]Use integration by parts to find
    \begin{equation*} \int \ln(x) \, dx. \end{equation*}
    At first glance we can’t integrate by parts, because there aren’t two parts! But note that we can write
    \begin{equation*} \ln(x) = \ln(x) \cdot 1, \end{equation*}
    and then set
    \begin{equation*} F(x) = \ln(x), \qquad \qquad g(x) = 1. \end{equation*}
    This implies
    \begin{equation*} f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad G(x) = x, \end{equation*}
    and the integration by parts formula now gives us
    \begin{align*} int \ln(x) \, dx \amp= x \cdot \ln(x) - \int x \cdot \dfrac{1}{x} \, dx\\ [4pt] \amp= x \cdot \ln(x) - \int 1 \, dx\\ [4pt] \amp= x \cdot \ln(x) - x + C. \end{align*}
    🔗
    We thus see that integration by parts—like integration by substitution—is an art rather than a set routine.

    Aside
    The ingredients of a successful integration by parts
    🔗
    If integration by parts is to work, several things must happen. First, you need to see that the method might actually apply. (In [[EX:2]] this wasn’t obvious.) Next, you need to identify the parts of the integrand that will be differentiated and integrated, respectively. The wrong choices can lead you away from a solution, rather than towards one. (See [[EX:3]] below for a cautionary tale.) Finally, you need to be able to carry out the integration of the new integral \(\int f \cdot G \, dx\text{.}\) As you work you may have to reshape the integrand algebraically. Technique comes with practice, and luck is useful, too.
    🔗
    [[EX:3]]Use integration by parts to find
    \begin{equation*} \int t \cdot e^t \, dt. \end{equation*}
    Set
    \begin{equation*} F(t) = e^t, \qquad \mbox \qquad g(t) = t; \end{equation*}
    then
    \begin{equation*} f(t) = e^t, \qquad \mbox \qquad G(t) = \dfrac{t^2}{2}. \end{equation*}
    The integration by parts formula then gives
    \begin{equation*} \int t \cdot e^t \, dt = \dfrac{t^2}{2} e^t - \int \dfrac{t^2}{2} e^t\,dt. \end{equation*}
    While this is a true statement, we are not better off—the new integral is not simpler than the original.

    \begin{align*} int \sin^2{\alpha x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{x}{2} - \dfrac{1}{4\alpha}\sin{2\alpha x} + C,\\ \int \cos^2{\alpha x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{x}{2} + \dfrac{1}{4\alpha}\sin{2\alpha x} + C,\\ \int \sin{\alpha x}\cos{\alpha x}\,dx \amp= - \dfrac{1}{4\alpha}\cos{2\alpha x} + C. \end{align*}
    In the remaining four, \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\) are different constants:
    \begin{align*} int \sin{\alpha x} \sin{\beta x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{\beta^2-\alpha^2} \left(\alpha\cos{\alpha x}\sin{\beta x} - \beta\sin{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\right) + C,\\ \int \cos{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{\beta^2-\alpha^2} \left(\beta\cos{\alpha x}\sin{\beta x} - \alpha\sin{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\right) + C,\\ \int \sin{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{\beta^2-\alpha^2} \left(\beta\sin{\alpha x}\sin{\beta x} + \alpha\cos{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\right) + C,\\ \int \cos{\alpha x} \sin{\beta x}\,dx \amp= \dfrac{1}{\beta^2-\alpha^2} \left(-\alpha\sin{\alpha x}\sin{\beta x} - \beta\cos{\alpha x} \cos{\beta x}\right) + C. \end{align*}

    12.

    In the later exercises we shall make frequent use of the following “trigonometric identities”:
    \begin{align*} 2 \sin{\alpha x} \cos{\alpha x} \amp= \sin{2\alpha x},\\ \cos^2{\alpha x} - \sin^2{\alpha x} \amp= \cos{2\alpha x},\\ \sin^2{\alpha x} + \cos^2{\alpha x} \amp= 1. \end{align*}
    Using a graphing package on a computer, graph together the functions
    \begin{equation*} 2 \sin{\alpha x} \cos{\alpha x} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \sin{2\alpha x} \end{equation*}
    to show that they seem to be identical. (That is, show that they “share phosphor.”) Then do the same for the pairs of functions in the other two identities.
    🔗
    (a)
    We can give a different argument for the identities above using the ideas we have developed in studying initial value problems. To prove the first identity, for instance, let \(f(x) = 2 \sin{\alpha x} \cos{\alpha x}\text{,}\) and let \(g(x) = \sin{2\alpha x}\text{.}\) Show that both functions satisfy
    \begin{equation*} y'' = -4\alpha^2 y, \quad\mbox{with}\quad y(0)=0\quad\mbox{and}\quad y'(0)=2\alpha. \end{equation*}
    Hence conclude the two functions must be the same.
    🔗

    (b)
    Find an initial value problem that is satisfied by both \(f(x)=\cos^2{\alpha x} - \sin^2{\alpha x}\) and by \(g(x) = \cos{2\alpha x}\text{.}\)
