    Section 10.4 Power Series and Differential Equations

    So far, we have begun with functions we already know, in the sense of being able to calculate the value of the function and all its derivatives at at least one point. This in turn allowed us to write down the corresponding Taylor series. Often, though, we don’t even have this much information about a function. In such cases it is frequently useful to assume that there is some infinite polynomial—called a power series—which represents the function, and then see if we can determine the coefficients of the polynomial.
    🔗
    This technique is especially useful in dealing with differential equations. To see why this is the case, think of the alternatives. If we can approximate the solution \(y=y(x)\) to a certain differential equation to an acceptable degree of accuracy by, say, a 20-th degree polynomial, then the only storage space required is the insignificant space taken to keep track of the 21 coefficients. Whenever we want the value of the solution for a given value of \(x\text{,}\) we can then get a quick approximation by evaluating the polynomial at \(x\text{.}\) Other alternatives are much more costly in time or in space. We could use Euler’s method to grind out the solution at \(x\text{,}\) but, as you’ve already discovered, this can be a slow and tedious process. Another option is to calculate lots of values and store them in a table in the computer’s memory. This not only takes up a lot of memory space, but it also only gives values for a finite set of values of \(x\text{,}\) and is not much faster than evaluating a polynomial. Until 30 years ago, the table approach was the standard one—all scientists and mathematicians had a handbook of mathematical functions containing hundreds of pages of numbers giving the values of every function they might need.
    🔗
    To see how this can happen, let’s first look at a familiar differential equation whose solutions we already know:
    \begin{equation*} y'=y. \end{equation*}
    Of course, we know by now that the solutions are \(y=ae^x\) for an arbitrary constant \(a\) (where \(a=y(0)\)). Suppose, though, that we didn’t already know how to solve this differential equation. We might see if we can find a power series of the form
    \begin{equation*} y = a_0 + a_1\,x + a_2\, x^2 + a_3\, x^3 + \cdots + a_n\, x^n + \cdots \end{equation*}
    that solves the differential equation. Can we, in fact, determine values for the coefficients \(a_0, a_1, a_2, \cdots , a_n , \cdots\) that will make \(y'=y\text{?}\)
    🔗
    Using the rules for differentiation, we have
    \begin{equation*} y'= a_1 + 2a_2\,x + 3 a_3\,x^2 + 4 a_4\,x^3 + \cdots + n a_n\,x^{n-1} + \cdots . \end{equation*}
    Two polynomials are equal if and only if the coefficients of corresponding powers of \(x\) are equal. Therefore, if \(y'=y\,,\) it would have to be true that
    \begin{align*} a_1 \amp= a_0\\ 2 a_2 \amp= a_1\\ 3 a_3 \amp= a_2\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-5pt] n a_n \amp= a_{n-1}\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots \end{align*}
    Therefore the values of \(a_1\text{,}\) \(a_2\text{,}\) \(a_3\text{,}\) …are not arbitrary; indeed, each is determined by the preceding one. Equations like these—which deal with a sequence of quantities and relate each term to those earlier in the sequence—are called recursion relations. These recursion relations permit us to express every \(a_n\) in terms of \(a_0\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rclclcl} a_1 &=& a_0 &&&& \\[3pt] a_2 &=& \dfrac{1}{2} \, a_1 & & &=& \dfrac{1}{2} \, a_0 \\[10pt] a_3 &=& \dfrac{1}{3} \, a_2 &=& \dfrac{1}{3}\cdot \dfrac{1}{2} \, a_0 &=& \dfrac{1}{3!} \, a_0 \\[10pt] a_4 &=& \dfrac{1}{4} \, a_3 &=& \dfrac{1}{4}\cdot\dfrac{1}{3!} \, a_0 &=& \dfrac{1}{4!} \, a_0 \\ &\vdots& &\vdots& &\vdots& \\ a_n &=& \dfrac{1}{n} \, a_{n-1}\ &=& \dfrac{1}{n}\cdot\dfrac{1}{(n-1)!} \, a_0 &=& \dfrac{1}{n!} \, a_0 \\[-5pt] &\vdots& &\vdots& &\vdots& \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Notice that \(a_0\) remains “free": there is no equation that determines its value. Thus, without additional information, \(a_0\) is arbitrary. The series for \(y\) now becomes
    \begin{equation*} y = a_0 + a_0\, x + \dfrac{1}{2!} \, a_0 \,x^2 + \dfrac{1}{3!} \, a_0 \,x^3 + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{n!} \, a_0 \,x^n + \cdots \end{equation*}
    or
    \begin{equation*} y = a_0\left[1 + x + \dfrac{1}{2!} \, x^2 + \dfrac{1}{3!} \, x^3 + \cdots + \dfrac{1}{n!} \, x^2 + \cdots \right]. \end{equation*}
    But the series in square brackets is just the Taylor series for \(e^x \,\)—we have derived the Taylor series from the differential equation alone, without using any of the other properties of the exponential function. Thus, we again find that the solutions of the differential equation \(y'=y\) are
    \begin{equation*} y=a_0e^x, \end{equation*}
    where \(a_0\) is an arbitrary constant. Notice that \(y(0)=a_0\text{,}\) so the value of \(a_0\) will be determined if the initial value of \(y\) is specified.
    🔗

    Note.

    In general, once we have derived a power series expression for a function, that power series will also be the Taylor series for that function. Although the two series are the same, the term Taylor series is typically reserved for those settings where we were able to evaluate the derivatives through some other means, as in the preceding section.
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 10.4.1 Bessel’s Equation

    🔗
    For a new example, let’s look at a differential equation that arises in an enormous variety of physical problems (wave motion, optics, the conduction of electricity and of heat and fluids, and the stability of columns, to name a few):
    \begin{equation*} x^2 \cdot y'' + x \cdot y' + (x^2 - p^2) \cdot y = 0 \, . \end{equation*}
    This is called the Bessel equation of order \(p\text{.}\) Here \(p\) is a parameter specified in advance, so we will really have a different set of solutions for each value of \(p\text{.}\) To determine a solution completely, we will also need to specify the initial values of \(y(0)\) and \(y'(0)\text{.}\) The solutions of the Bessel equation of order \(p\) are called Bessel functions of order \(p\text{,}\) and the solution for a given value of \(p\) (together with particular initial conditions which needn’t concern us here) is written \(J_p(x)\text{.}\) In general, there is no formula for a Bessel function in terms of simpler functions (although it turns out that a few special cases like \(J_{1/2}(x), J_{3/2}(x) , \ldots\) can be expressed relatively simply). To evaluate such a function we could use Euler’s method, or we could try to find a power series solution.
    🔗

    Aside
    Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel (1784–1846) was a German astronomer who studied the functions that now bear his name in his efforts to analyze the perturbations of planetary motions, particularly those of Saturn.
    🔗
    Consider the Bessel equation with \(p=0\text{.}\) We are thus trying to solve the differential equation
    \begin{equation*} x^2 \cdot y'' + x \cdot y' + x^2 \cdot y = 0. \end{equation*}
    By dividing by \(x\text{,}\) we can simplify this a bit to
    \begin{equation*} x \cdot y'' + y' + x \cdot y = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Let’s look for a power series expansion
    \begin{equation*} y = b_0 + b_1\,x + b_2\,x^2 +b_3\,x^3 + \cdots . \end{equation*}
    We have
    \begin{align*} y' \amp= b_1 + 2b_2x + 3b_3x^2 + 4b_4x^3 + \cdots + (n+1)b_{n+1}x^n +\cdots \intertext{and} y'' \amp= 2b_2 + 6b_3x + 12b_4x^2 + 20b_5x^3 + \cdots + (n+2)(n+1)b_{n+2}x^n + \cdots , \end{align*}
    We can now use these expressions to calculate the series for the combination that occurs in the differential equation:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rcccccccl} xy''&=& & &2b_2\,x&+&6b_3\,x^2&+&\cdots \\ y'&=&b_1 & +&2b_2\,x&+&3b_3\,x^2&+&\cdots \\ xy&=& & &b_0\,x&+&b_1\,x^2&+&\cdots \\[1pt] \hline \\ [-10pt] xy''+y'+xy&=&b_1&+&(4b_2+b_0 )x&+&(9b_3+b_1)x^2&+&\cdots \end{array} \end{equation*}
    In general,

    Aside
    Finding the coefficient of \(x^n\)
    🔗
    the coefficient of \(x^n\) in the combination will be
    \begin{equation*} (n+1)n\, b_{n+1} + (n+1)\, b_{n+1} +b_{n-1} = (n+1)^2\, b_{n+1} +b_{n-1}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If the power series \(y\) is to be a solution to the original differential equation, the infinite series for \(xy''+y'+xy\) must equal 0. This in turn means that every coefficient of that series must be 0. We thus get
    \begin{align*} b_1 \amp= 0,\\ 4b_2+b_0 \amp= 0,\\ 9b_3+b_1 \amp= 0,\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-5pt] n^2b_n +b_{n-2} \amp= 0.\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots \end{align*}
    If we now solve these recursively as before, we see first off that since \(b_1=0\text{,}\) it must also be true that
    \begin{equation*} b_k=0 \qquad \text{for every odd $k$}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    For the even coefficients we have
    \begin{align*} b_2\amp=-\frac{1}{2^2}b_0,\\ b_4\amp=-\frac{1}{4^2}b_2= \frac{1}{2^24^2}b_0,\\ b_6\amp=-\frac{1}{6^2}b_4=-\frac{1}{2^2 4^2 6^2}b_0, \end{align*}
    and, in general,
    \begin{equation*} b_{2n} = \pm \frac{1}{2^2 4^2 6^2 \cdots (2n)^2}b_0 = \pm \frac{1}{2^{2n} (n!)^2}b_0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Thus any function \(y\) satisfying the Bessel equation of order 0 must be of the form
    \begin{equation*} y = b_0\left(1-\frac{x^2}{2^2}+\frac{x^4}{2^4(2!)^2} - \frac{x^6}{2^6(3!)^2} +\cdots \right). \end{equation*}
    In particular, if we impose the initial condition \(y(0)=1\) (which requires that \(b_0=1\)), we get the 0-th order Bessel function \(J_0(x)\text{:}\)
    \begin{equation*} J_0(x) = 1 - \frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{x^4}{64} - \frac{x^6}{2304} + \frac{x^8}{147456}+\cdots . \end{equation*}

    In exactly the same way, we can find power series solutions when there are several interacting variables involved. Let’s look at the example we’ve considered at a number of points in this text to see how this works. In the

    Aside
    The \(S\)-\(I\)-\(R\) model
    🔗
    \(S\)-\(I\)-\(R\) model we basically wanted to solve the system of equations
    \begin{align*} S'\amp= -a SI,\\ I'\amp= aSI - bI,\\ R'\amp= bI , \end{align*}
    where \(a\) and \(b\) were parameters depending on the specific situation. Let’s look for solutions of the form
    \begin{align*} S\amp= s_0 + s_1\,t + s_2\,t^2 + s_3\,t^3 + \cdots ,\\ I\amp= i_0 + i_1\,t + i_2\,t^2 + i_3\,t^3 + \cdots ,\\ R\amp= r_0 + r_1\,t + r_2\,t^2 + r_3\,t^3 + \cdots . \end{align*}
    🔗
    If we put these series in the equation \(S'= -a\, S\, I\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{align*} s_1 + 2s_2t + 3s_3t^2 + \cdots \amp= -a(s_0 + s_1t + s_2t^2 + \cdots)(i_0 + i_1t + i_2t^2 + \cdots)\\ \amp= -a(s_0i_0 + (s_0i_1+s_1i_0)t+(s_0i_2+s_1i_1+s_2i_0)t^2 + \cdots ). \end{align*}
    🔗
    As before,

    Aside
    Finding the coefficients of the power series for \(S(t)\)
    🔗
    if the two sides of the differential equation are to be equal, the coefficients of corresponding powers of \(t\) must be equal:
    \begin{align*} s_1 \amp= -as_0i_0,\\ 2s_2 \amp= -a(s_0i_1+s_1i_0),\\ 3s_3 \amp= -a(s_0i_2+s_1i_1+s_2i_0),\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-5pt] ns_n \amp= -a(s_0i_{n-1}+s_1i_{n-2}+ \ldots +s_{n-2}i_1+s_{n-1}i_0)\\ [-5pt] \amp\ \, \vdots\\ [-5pt] \end{align*}
    🔗
    While this looks messy, it has the crucial recursive feature—each \(s_k\) is expressed in terms of previous terms.

    Aside
    Recursion again
    🔗
    That is, if we knew all the \(s\) and the \(i\) coefficients out through the coefficients of, say, \(t^6\) in the series for \(S\) and \(I\text{,}\) we could immediately calculate \(s_7\text{.}\) We again have a recursion relation.
    🔗
    We could expand the equation \(I'= aSI - bI\) in the same way,

    Aside
    Finding the power series for \(I(t)\)
    🔗
    and get recursion relations for the coefficients \(i_k\text{.}\) In this model, though, there is a shortcut if we observe that since \(S'= -aSI\text{,}\) and since \(I'= aSI - bI\text{,}\) we have \(I' = -S' -bI\text{.}\) If we substitute the power series in this expression and equate coefficients, we get
    \begin{equation*} ni_n = -ns_n - bi_{n-1}, \end{equation*}
    which leads to
    \begin{equation*} i_n = -s_n-\frac{b}{n}i_{n-1} \end{equation*}
    —so if we know \(s_n\) and \(i_{n-1}\text{,}\) we can calculate \(i_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We are now in a position to calculate the coefficients as far out as we like. For we will be given values for \(a\) and \(b\) when we are given the model. Moreover, since \(s_0=S(0) =\) the initial \(S\)-population, and \(i_0=I(0)=\) the initial \(I\)-population, we will also typically be given these values as well. But knowing \(s_0\) and \(i_0\text{,}\) we can determine \(s_1\) and then \(i_1\text{.}\) But then, knowing these values, we can determine \(s_2\) and then \(i_2\text{,}\) and so on. Since the arithmetic is tedious, this is obviously a place for a computer. Here is a program that calculates the first 50 coefficients in the power series for \(S(t)\) and \(I(t)\text{:}\)
    🔗
    Program: SIRSERIES
    🔗
    DIM S(0 to 50), I(0 to 50)
a = .00001
b = 1/14
S(0) = 45400
I(0) = 2100
FOR k = 1 TO 50
     Sum = 0
     FOR j = 0 TO k - 1
          Sum = Sum + S(j) * I(k - j - 1)
     NEXT j
     S(k) = -a * SUM/k
     I(k) = -S(k) - b * I(k - 1)/k
NEXT k

    Comment:.

    The opening command in this program introduces a new feature. It notifies the computer that the variables S and I are going to be arrays—strings of numbers—and that each array will consist of 51 elements. The element S(k) corresponds to what we have been calling \(s_k\text{.}\) The integer k is called the index of the term in the array. The indices in this program run from 0 to 50.
    🔗
    The effect of running this program is thus to create two 51-element arrays, S and I, containing the coefficients of the power series for \(S\) and \(I\) out to degree 50. If we just wanted to see these coefficients, we could have the computer list them. Here are the first 35 coefficients for \(S\) (read across the rows):

    🔗
    In the same fashion, we find that the power series for \(I\) begins
    \begin{equation*} 2100 +803.4t + 143.66824t^2 + 14.561389t^3 + .60966648t^4 + \cdots + 2.021195\times 10^{-31}t^{34} + \cdots \end{equation*}

    7.

    Legendre’s differential equation
    \begin{equation*} (1 - x^2)y'' - 2xy' + \ell(\ell + 1)y = 0 \end{equation*}
    arises in many physical problems—for example, in quantum mechanics, where its solutions are used to describe certain orbits of the electron in a hydrogen atom. In that context, the parameter \(\ell\) is called the angular momentum of the electron; it must be either an integer or a “half-integer” (i.e., a number like 3/2). Quantum theory gets its name from the fact that numbers like the angular momentum of the electron in the hydrogen atom are “quantized", that is, they cannot have just any value, but must be a multiple of some “quantum"—in this case, the number 1/2.
