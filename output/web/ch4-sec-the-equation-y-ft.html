    Section 4.5 The Equation \(y' = f(t)\)

    Most differential equations we have encountered express the rate of growth of a quantity in terms of the quantity itself. The simplest models for biological growth had this form: \(y' = ky\) and \(y' = ky^p\text{.}\) Even when several variables were present—as in the \(S\)-\(I\)-\(R\) model and the predator-prey models—it was most natural to express the rates at which those variables change in terms of the variables themselves. Even the motion of a spring (Exercise Group 4.2.4.24–26) was described that way: the rate of change of position equalled the velocity, and the rate of change of velocity was proportional to the position.
    🔗
    Sometimes, though, a differential equation

    Aside
    The motion of a falling body …
    🔗
    will express the rate of change of a variable directly in terms of the input variable. For example, on page  we saw that the velocity \(dx/dt\) of a body falling under the sole influence of gravity is a linear function of the time:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dx}{dt} = -gt + v_0. \end{equation*}
    Here \(x\) is the height of the body above the ground, \(g\) is the acceleration due to gravity, and \(v_0\) is the velocity at time \(t = 0\text{.}\) This equation has the general form
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dx}{dt} = f(t), \end{equation*}
    where \(f(t)\) is a given function of \(t\text{.}\) We will now consider special methods that can be used to study differential equations of this special form.
    🔗

    Subsection 4.5.1 Antiderivatives

    To solve the equation of motion of a body

    Aside
    …and its solution
    🔗
    falling under gravity, we must find a function \(x(t)\) whose derivative is given as
    \begin{equation*} x' = -gt + v_0. \end{equation*}
    We can call upon our knowledge of the rules of differentiation to find \(x\text{.}\) Consider \(-gt\) first.

    Aside
    \(-gt\) is the derivative of \(-gt^2/2\)
    🔗
    What function has \(-gt\) as its derivative? We can start with \(t^2\text{,}\) whose derivative is \(2t\text{.}\) Since we want the derivative to turn out to be \(-gt\text{,}\) we can reason this way:
    \begin{equation*} -gt = -\dfrac{g}{2} \cdot 2t = -\dfrac{g}{2} \times \mbox{the derivative of $t^2$}. \end{equation*}
    This leads us to identify \(-gt^2/2\) as a function whose derivative is \(-gt\text{.}\) Check for yourself that this is correct by differentiating \(-gt^2/2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Now consider \(v_0\text{,}\) the other part of \(dx/dt\text{.}\) What function has the constant \(v_0\) as its derivative?

    Aside
    \(v_0\) is the derivative of \(v_0t\) and of \(v_0t + b\)
    🔗
    A derivative is a rate of growth, and we know that the linear functions are precisely the ones that have constant growth rates. Furthermore, the rate is the multiplier for a linear function, so we conclude that any linear function of the form \(v_0 t + b\) has derivative \(v_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    If we put the two pieces together, we find that
    \begin{equation*} x(t) = -\dfrac{g}{2} \, t^2 + v_0 t + b \end{equation*}
    is a solution to the differential equation, for any value of \(b\text{.}\) (Recall from Section 4.2 that a differential equation can have many solutions.) We constructed this formula for \(x(t)\) by “undoing”

    Aside
    The antiderivative of a function
    🔗
    the process of differentiation, a process sometimes called antidifferentiation. The function produced is called an antiderivative. Thus:
    The function
    \begin{equation*} -\dfrac{g}{2}t^2 + v_0t + b \end{equation*}
    is an antiderivative of \(-gt+v_0\text{.}\)
    🔗
    This is true because \(-gt+v_0\) is the derivative of \(-\dfrac{g}{2}t^2 + v_0t + b\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Note that a function

    Aside
    All the functions \(F(x) +C\) are antiderivatives of \(F'(x)\)
    🔗
    has only one derivative, but it has many antiderivatives. If \(F(t)\) is an antiderivative of \(f(t)\text{,}\) then so is \(F(t) + C\text{,}\) where \(C\) is any constant.
    🔗
    The list of functions and their derivatives that we compiled in Chapter 3 can be “turned around” to become a list of functions and their antiderivatives. Note that the antiderivative column should really be labelled “an antiderivative” since we could add a constant to any of the listed functions and still have an antiderivative for the function in the first column.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{lc} \text{function} & \text{antiderivative} \\[2pt] \hline 0 & c \\ x^p & \dfrac{1}{p+1}x^{p+1} \text{ (if } p\neq -1\text{)} \\ x^{-1} & \ln x \\ \sin x & -\cos x \\ \cos x & \sin x \\ \exp x=e^x & \exp x=e^x \\ b^x & \dfrac{1}{\ln b}\,b^x \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Notice the formula for the antiderivative of \(x^p\)

    Aside
    Every power of \(x\) has an antiderivative
    🔗
    requires \(p+1 \neq 0\text{,}\) that is, \(p \neq -1\text{.}\) This leaves out \(x^{-1}\text{.}\) However, the antiderivative of \(x^{-1}\) is \(\ln{x}\text{,}\) so no power of \(x\) is excluded from the table.
    🔗
    We also had differentiation rules that told us how to deal with different combinations of functions. Each of these rules has an analogue in antidifferentiation. The simplest combinations are a sum and a constant multiple.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{cc} \mbox{function} & \mbox{antiderivative} \\ \hline \rule{0pt}{14pt} f(x) \rule{0pt}{14pt} & F(x) \\ g(x) & G(x) \\ c \cdot f(x) & c \cdot F(x) \\ f(x) + g(x) & F(x) + G(x) \end{array} \end{equation*}
    We defer a discussion of the analogue of the chain rule to second semester calculus, when you will learn about techniques of integration.
    🔗
    With just these rules we can find the antiderivative of any polynomial, for instance. (Recall that a polynomial is a sum of constant multiples of powers of the input variable.) Here is a collection of sample antiderivatives that illustrate the various rules. To emphasize the fact that antiderivatives are determined only up to an additive constant, various constants have been tacked on—any other constant would work just as well. You should compare this table with the one in Table 3.5.8.

    Subsection 4.5.2 Euler’s Method Revisited

    If we know the formula for an antiderivative of \(f(t)\text{,}\) then we can write down a solution to the differential equation \(dy/dt = f(t)\text{.}\) For example, the general solution to
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = 12t^2 + \sin{t} \end{equation*}
    is \(y = 4t^3 - \cos{t} + C\text{.}\) In such a case we have a shortcut to solving the differential equation without needing to use Euler’s method. Often, though, there is no formula for an antiderivative of \(f(t)\)—even when \(f(t)\) itself has a simple formula. There is no formula for the antiderivative of \(\cos(t^2)\text{,}\) or \(\sin{t}/t\text{,}\) or \(\sqrt{1 + t^3}\text{,}\) for instance. In other cases, \(f(t)\) may not even be given by a formula. It may be a data function, given as a graph made by a pen tracing on a moving sheet of graph paper.
    🔗
    Whether we can find a formula for an antiderivative of \(f(t)\) or not, we can still solve the differential equation \(dy/dt = f(t)\) by Euler’s method. It turns out that Euler’s method takes on a relatively simple form in such cases. Let’s investigate this in the following context.
    🔗
    Let \(V\) be the volume of water in a reservoir

    Aside
    The volume of a reservoir varies over time
    🔗
    serving a small town, measured in millions of gallons. Then \(V\) is a function of the time \(t\text{,}\) measured in days. Rainfall adds water to the reservoir, while evaporation and consumption by the townspeople take it away. Let \(f\) be the net rate at which water is flowing into the reservoir, in millions of gallons per day. Sometimes \(f\) will be positive—when rainfall exceeds evaporation and consumption—and sometimes \(f\) will be negative. The net inflow rate varies from day to day; that is, \(f\) is a function of time: \(f~=~f(t)\text{.}\) Our model of the reservoir is the differential equation
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dV}{dt} = f(t) \ \mbox{millions of gallons per day}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Suppose \(f(t)\) is measured every two days,

    Aside
    The net inflow rate
    🔗
    and those measurements are recorded in the following table.
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rc} \text{time } t & \text{rate } f(t) \\ \text{(days)} & (10^6 \times \text{ gals.\ per day}) \\ \hline 0 & \phantom{-}0.34 \\ 2 & \phantom{-}0.11 \\ 4 & -0.07 \\ 6 & -0.23 \\ 8 & -0.14 \\ 10 & \phantom{-}0.03 \\ 12 & \phantom{-}0.08 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    Note that in this table we are able to write down the rate for all values of \(t\) immediately, without having to calculate the intermediate values of the dependent variable \(V\text{.}\) This is in marked contrast with most of the examples we’ve looked at in this course where we had to know the values of all the variables for any time \(t\) before we could calculate the new rate value at that time. It is this simplification that gives differential equations of the form \(y' = f(t)\) their special structure.
    🔗
    If we assume the value of \(f(t)\) remains constant for the two days after each measurement is made, we can approximate the total change in \(V\) over these 14 days. The following table does this; it tells us how much \(V\) changes over each two-day period, and also

    Aside
    The accumulated change in \(V\)
    🔗
    the total accumulated change in \(V\) by the end that period. Since \(\Delta t = 2\) days, we calculate \(\Delta V\) by
    \begin{equation*} \Delta V = V' \cdot \Delta t = f(t) \cdot \Delta t = 2 \cdot f(t). \end{equation*}
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrr} \text{starting} & \text{current} & \text{accumulated} & \text{ending} \\ t & \Delta V & \Delta V & t \\[2pt] \hline 0 & \phantom{-}0.68 & \phantom{-}0.68 & 2 \\ 2 & \phantom{-}0.22 & \phantom{-}0.90 & 4 \\ 4 & -0.14 & \phantom{-}0.76 & 6 \\ 6 & -0.46 & \phantom{-}0.30 & 8 \\ 8 & -0.28 & \phantom{-}0.02 & 10 \\ 10 & \phantom{-}0.06 & \phantom{-}0.08 & 12 \\ 12 & \phantom{-}0.16 & \phantom{-}0.24 & 14 \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    At the end of the 14 days, \(V\) has accumulated a total change of 0.24 million gallons. Notice this does not depend on the initial size of \(V\text{.}\) If \(V\) had been 92.64 million gallons at the start, it would be 92.64 + 0.24 = 92.88 million gallons at the end. If it had been only 2 million gallons at the start, it would be 2 + 0.24 = 2.24 million gallons at the end. Other models do not behave this way: in two weeks, a rabbit population of 900 will change much more than a population of 90. The total change in \(V\) is independent of \(V\) because the rate at which \(V\) changes is independent of \(V\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We can therefore use Euler’s method

    Aside
    Calculating just the accumulated change in \(y\)
    🔗
    to solve any differential equation of the form \(dy/dt = f(t)\) independently of an initial value for \(y\text{.}\) We just calculate the total accumulated change in \(y\text{,}\) and add that total to any given initial \(y\text{.}\) Here is how it works when the initial value of \(t\) is \(a\text{,}\) and the time step is \(\Delta t\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{cccc} \text{starting} & \text{current} & \text{accumulated} & \text{ending} \\ t & \Delta y & \Delta y & t \\[2pt] \hline a & f(a)\,\Delta t & \begin{array}{c} \text{previously accumulated} \\ \Delta y+\text{ current }\Delta y \end{array} & a+\Delta t \\[12pt] a+\Delta t & f(a+\Delta t)\,\Delta t & \begin{array}{c} \text{previously accumulated} \\ \Delta y+\text{ current }\Delta y \end{array} & a+2\Delta t \\[12pt] a+2\Delta t & f(a+2\Delta t)\,\Delta t & \begin{array}{c} \text{previously accumulated} \\ \Delta y+\text{ current }\Delta y \end{array} & a+3\Delta t \\[12pt] a+3\Delta t & f(a+3\Delta t)\,\Delta t & \begin{array}{c} \text{previously accumulated} \\ \Delta y+\text{ current }\Delta y \end{array} & a+4\Delta t \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \vdots \\ a+(n-1)\Delta t & f(a+(n-1)\Delta t)\,\Delta t & \begin{array}{c} \text{previously accumulated} \\ \Delta y+\text{ current }\Delta y \end{array} & a+n\Delta t \end{array} \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The third column is too small to hold the

    Aside
    The accumulated change in \(y\)
    🔗
    values of “accumulated \(\Delta y\text{.}\)” Instead, it contains the instructions for obtaining those values. It says: to get the current value of “accumulated \(\Delta y\text{,}\)” add the “current \(\Delta y\)” to the previous value of “accumulated \(\Delta y\text{.}\)”
    🔗
    Let’s use Euler’s method to find the accumulated \(\Delta y\) when \(t = 4\text{,}\) given that
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = \cos(t^2) \end{equation*}
    and \(t\) is initially 0. If we use 8 steps, then \(\Delta t = 0.5\) and we obtain the following:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rrrr} \text{starting} & \text{current} & \text{accumulated} & \text{ending} \\ t & \Delta y & \Delta y & t \\[2pt] \hline 0.0 & \phantom{-}0.5000 & \phantom{-}0.5000 & 0.5 \\ 0.5 & \phantom{-}0.4845 & \phantom{-}0.9845 & 1.0 \\ 1.0 & \phantom{-}0.2702 & \phantom{-}1.2546 & 1.5 \\ 1.5 & -0.3141 & \phantom{-}0.9405 & 2.0 \\ 2.0 & -0.3268 & \phantom{-}0.6137 & 2.5 \\ 2.5 & \phantom{-}0.4997 & \phantom{-}1.1134 & 3.0 \\ 3.0 & -0.4556 & \phantom{-}0.6579 & 3.5 \\ 3.5 & \phantom{-}0.4752 & \phantom{-}1.1330 & 4.0 \end{array} \end{equation*}

    🔗
    Let’s compare our reservoir model with population growth. The rate at which a population grows depends, in an obvious way, on the size of the population. By contrast, the rate at which the reservoir fills does not depend on how much water there is in the reservoir.

    Aside
    Exogenous and endogenous factors
    🔗
    It depends on factors outside the reservoir: rainfall and consumption. These factors are said to be exogenous (from the Greek exo-, “outside” and -gen, “produced,” or “born”). The opposite is called an endogenous factor (from the Greek endo-, “within”). Evaporation is an endogenous factor for the reservoir model; population size is certainly an endogenous factor for a population model.
    🔗
    Precisely because exogenous factors are “outside the system,” we need to be given the information on how they vary over time. In the reservoir model, this information appears in the function \(f(t)\) that describes the rate at which \(V\) changes. In general, if \(y\) depends on exogenous factors that vary over time, we can expect the differential equation for \(y\) to involve a function of time:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{dy}{dt} = f(t) \end{equation*}
    Thus, we can view this section as dealing with models that involve exogenous factors.
    🔗

    Aside
    The differential equation of motion for a falling body, \(dx/dt = -gt + v_0\text{,}\) indicates that gravity is an exogenous factor. In Greek and medieval European science, the reason an object fell to the ground was assumed to lie within the object itself—it was the object’s “heaviness.” By making the cause of motion exogenous, rather than endogenous, Galileo and Newton started a scientific revolution.
