    Section 9.3 Optimization

    Optimization is the process of making the best choice from a range of possibilities. (“Optimum” is the Latin word for best.)

    Aside
    The contexts for optimization
    🔗
    We are all familiar with optimization in the economic arena: managers of an enterprise typically seek to to maximize profit or minimize cost by making conscious choices. It is perhaps more surprising to learn that we sometimes use the same language to describe physical processes. For instance, the atoms in a molecule are arranged so that their total energy is minimized. A light ray travels from one point to another along the path that takes the least time. Of course atoms and photons don’t make conscious choices. Nevertheless, the imagery of optimization is so vivid and useful that we try to invoke it whenever we can.
    🔗
    Usually, there is a restriction—called a constraint—on the choices that can be made to achieve to best possible outcome.

    Aside
    Constrained optimization
    🔗
    For instance, consider a factory that makes tennis rackets. We can expect that the factory managers are instructed to minimize cost while producing a given number of rackets. This is their constraint. Production cost is a function of many quantities that the managers can control—the number of workers, the wage scale, and the cost of the raw materials are just a few. When the managers choose values for these quantities that minimize the cost function, they must be sure those values will also satisfy the constraint.
    🔗
    In mathematical terms, optimization

    Aside
    Mathematical optimization
    🔗
    is the process of finding the minimum or maximum value of a function. The presence of constraints complicates this task, as you shall see.
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.1 Visual Inspection

    The maximum value of a function is the highest point on its graph; the minimum value is the lowest. Shown at the right is the graph of \(z = x^3 - 4x - y^2\) on the domain
    🔗
    \(-2 \leq x \leq 3 \qquad -2 \leq y \leq 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The maximum occurs where \((x, y) = (3, 0)\text{,}\) and it has the value \(z = 15\text{.}\) The minimum is \(z \approx -12.1\text{,}\) when \((x, y) \approx (1.2, 3)\text{.}\) Confirm this yourself by inspecting the graph and then calculating \(z\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We’ll use the term extreme

    Aside
    Extremes, and local extremes
    🔗
    to refer to either a maximum or a minimum. In the example we see several local extremes. These are points where the value of the function is larger or smaller than it is at any nearby point. There are local minima at both left-hand corners of the graph, at \((-2, -2)\) and \((-2, 3)\text{.}\) There is another along the front edge, near \((1.2, -2)\text{.}\) There is a local maximum in the interior, near \((-1.2, 0)\text{.}\) To decide which local minimum is the true minimum, we must simply look. It is the same for local maxima.
    🔗
    In our example,

    Aside
    The domain is a constraint
    🔗
    the domain of definition of the function acts as a constraint. If we change the domain, the positions of the extreme points can change. For example, suppose we change the position of the right-hand border in stages: first, \(x \leq 3\text{;}\) second, \(x \leq 2.5\text{;}\) third, \(x \leq 2\text{.}\) (Since the graph itself doesn’t change, we use a grid to show the part of the graph that satisfies
    🔗
    the constraint in each case below.) At the start, the maximum is on the boundary. When we first move the boundary to the left (from \(x = 3\) to \(x = 2.5\)),

    Aside
    The maximum moves —it even jumps
    🔗
    the maximum just moves with it. However, when we move the boundary farther (from \(x = 2.5\) to \(x = 2\)), the maximum jumps from the boundary to an interior point (near \((-1.2, 0)\)). During these changes the minimum is not affected. It stays at the same place.
    🔗
    The sudden jump in the maximum

    Aside
    Catastrophes
    🔗
    is called a catastrophe. The figures explain what happens. We impose a constraint \(x \leq a\text{,}\) and then we reduce the value of the parameter \(a\text{.}\) At first, the maximum is at the boundary point \((a, 0)\text{.}\) The position of this point changes smoothly with \(a\text{,}\) causing a gradual drop in the value of the maximum. Eventually, the maximum reaches the same value as the interior local maximum. (This happens when \(a = 4/\sqrt{3}\text{;}\) see the exercises.) If \(a\) continues to decrease, the local maximum at \((a, 0)\) then has a lower value than the local maximum at the interior point, so the true maximum jumps to the interior.
    🔗
    There are many variations on this pattern. Whenever a function depends on a parameter the positions of its extremes do, too. There are many ways for the position of an extreme to jump suddenly while the parameter is changing gradually. Any such jump is called a catastrophe.
    🔗

    Aside
    Catastrophes make the task of optimization more interesting. If the maximum of a certain function gives the optimal solution to a problem, and that maximum jumps to a new position, then the optimal solution changes radically. For example, suppose the problem is to determine the minimum-energy configuration of atoms in a molecule. When the minimum jumps catastrophically, there is a new configuration of the same atoms, producing an isomeric form of the molecule.
    🔗
    The quest for an optimum does not have to involve mathematical tools. A good example is John Stuart Mill’s philosophy of “the greatest good for the greatest number”. In politics a catastrophe is called a revolution. Even scientific research pursues an optimum in raising the question: “What is the best way to explain certain phenomena?” The consensus in the scientific community can change catastrophically, in what is called a paradigm shift or an intellectual revolution. The geological theory of plate tectonics is a familiar example. Though proposed in the 1920s, it was dismissed until the 1960s, when it was suddenly and overwhelmingly accepted.
    🔗

    Density plots.

    We can also solve optimization problems by inspecting density plots. Suppose \(z\) is the yield from a process that is controlled by two inputs \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) and
    \begin{equation*} z = 3xy - 2 y^2. \end{equation*}
    Initially we take \(0 \leq x \leq 4\text{,}\) \(0 \leq y \leq 4\text{.}\) The maximum yield is at the darkest spot in the upper density plot. It occurs on the right boundary, near \(y = 3\text{.}\)
    🔗
     The position of the maximum is subject to change if we have to impose further constraints. For instance, suppose the resource \(y\) is more limited than we first assumed, requiring us to set \(y \leq 2.3\text{.}\) With this added constraint we see that the maximum shifts to the corner \((x, y) = (4, 2.3)\text{.}\) (The points shown in a lighter gray are the ones that have been removed from consideration by the new constraint.)
    🔗
     Besides limits on individual resources, we are often faced with a limit on total resources. In our case, let’s suppose the limit has the form
    \begin{equation*} x + y \leq 4. \end{equation*}
    That means all points above the line \(x + y = 4\) must be removed from consideration. (They are shown in lighter gray.) This new constraint causes the maximum to shift yet again. It now appears near the point \((x, y) = (3, 1)\text{.}\)
    🔗
    As we add constraints that force the maximum to move, the density at each new maximum is less than it was at the previous one. Thus, the value of the maximum itself decreases.

    Aside
    Constraints reduce optimality
    🔗
    In other words, each added constraint makes the optimal solution slightly “less optimal” than it had been. This is only to be expected.
    🔗
    Of course the extremes may appear in the interior

    Aside
    Extremes on the interior of a density plot
    🔗
    of the domain as well as on the boundary—and density plots can show this. Here are the density plots that correspond to the graphs of \(z = x^3 - 4x - y^2\) that we saw on page . The maximum jumps to the interior when the value of \(a\) in the constraint \(x \leq a\)
    🔗
    drops below \(a = 4/\sqrt{3}\text{.}\) In all three plots the minimum appears as the bright spot at the top of the rectangle near where \(x = 1\text{.}\) (The exact location is \((x, y) = (2/\sqrt{3}, 3)\text{.}\)) We can also see a local minimum at the bottom of the rectangle near \(x = 1\text{.}\) From the graph we know there are two more at the left corners of the rectangle, but they are harder to notice in the density plots.
    🔗
    🔗

    Contour plots.

    A density plot is useful for showing the general location of the highs and lows, but we can get a more precise picture by switching to a contour plot. Let’s look at the contour plots of the same problems we just analyzed using density plots.
    🔗
     We’ll start with the function \(z = 3xy - 2 y^2\) we first considered on page  and search for its extremes subject to the constraints
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gather*} 0 \leq x, \\ 0 \leq y \leq 2.3, \\ x + y \leq 4, \end{gather*} \end{equation*}
    that we introduced earlier. From the density plot on page  it was obvious that the values of \(z\)
    🔗
    increase steadily from the upper left corner of the large square to the upper part of the right side. In the contour plot, though, we need some sort of labels to show us where the low and high values of \(z\) are to be found.
    🔗
    To locate the extremes within the constrained region, we need to find contours that carry the lowest and the highest values of \(z\text{.}\) We can do this quite precisely.

    Aside
    An extreme can occur where a contour is tangent to the boundary
    🔗
    The contour that just passes through the upper left corner—and meets the region at that point alone—carries the lowest value of \(z\text{.}\) If you study the plot you can see that the contour carrying the highest value of \(z\) also touches the boundary at just a single point. It is the contour that is tangent to the line \(x + y = 4\text{,}\) and elsewhere lies outside the constrained region.
    🔗
     Suppose the extreme is in the interior of the constrained region, rather than on the boundary. For example, the function \(z = x^3 - 4x - y^2\) has an interior maximum when
    \begin{equation*} \begin{gather*} -2 \leq x \leq 2 \\ -2 \leq y \leq 3. \end{gather*} \end{equation*}
    Around the maximum there is a nest of concentric ovals. This pattern is characteristic for an interior extreme. Notice the local maximum where a contour is tangent to the boundary line \(x = 2\text{.}\)
    🔗
    These examples demonstrate

    Aside
    Contour patterns at an extreme
    🔗
    that there are characteristic patterns that contour lines make near an extreme point. One pattern appears along a constraint curve; another pattern appears at interior points of a domain. We first
    🔗
    Patterns of contour lines at an extreme point
    🔗
    met the pattern that appears at an interior extreme point when we discussed the ‘standard’ minimum (\(x^2 + y^2\)) and maximum (\(-x^2 - y^2\)) in section 1 (pages –). Caution: the patterns you see here are “typical”, but they do not guarantee the presence of an extreme point. The exercises give you a chance to explore some of the subtleties.

    Subsection 9.3.2 Dimension-reducing Constraints

    Constraints appear frequently in optimization problems.

    Aside
    How a constraint can reduce the dimension of a problem
    🔗
    Thus far, however, the constraints we considered have been described by inequalities, like \(x + y \leq 4\text{.}\) Initially, \(x\) and \(y\) give us the coordinates of a point in a two-dimensional plane. The effect of the constraint \(x + y \leq 4\) is to restrict the points \((x, y)\) to just a part of that plane—but it is still a two-dimensional part. Sometimes, though, the constraint is given by an equality, like \(x + y = 4\text{.}\) In that case, the points \((x, y)\) are restricted to lie on a line—which is a one-dimensional set in the plane. The second constraint therefore reduces the dimension of the problem.
    🔗
    There is a standard form

    Aside
    A standard form
    🔗
    for any constraint that reduces a two-dimensional optimization problem to a one-dimensional problem. The form is this:
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{constraint:} \quad g(x, y) = 0. \end{equation*}
    For instance, the constraint \(x + y = 4\) can be written this way by setting \(g(x, y) = x + y - 4\text{.}\) We’ll look at another example in a moment.
    🔗
    First, though, notice that \(g(x, y) = 0\) is one of the contour lines of the function \(g(x, y)\text{,}\) namely, the contour at level zero. Since the contours of \(g\) are curves, we often call \(g(x, y) = 0\) a constraint curve.

    Aside
    A comment on dimension
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.3 Extremes and Critical Points

    Suppose that a function \(f(x, y)\) has a maximum or a minimum at an interior point \((a, b)\text{.}\) Suppose also that the function is locally linear at \((a, b)\text{,}\) so we have a “bowl” rather than a “spike” or some other irregularity in the graph. Then we must have
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{grad} f (a, b) = \left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b), \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) \right) = (0, 0). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Here is why. The gradient vector \(\mbox{grad} f (a, b)\)

    Aside
    A proof
    🔗
    tells us how the graph of \(z = f(x, y)\) is tilted at the point \((a, b)\text{.}\) (We discuss the geometric meaning of the gradient on page .) At a maximum or a minimum, though, the graph is not tilted; it must be flat. Therefore, the gradient must be the zero vector.
    🔗
    The ideas here carry over to functions

    Aside
    Moving to an arbitrary number of input variables
    🔗
    that have any number of input variables. First, we give a name to a point where the gradient is zero.
    🔗
    The observation we just made can now be restated as a theorem that connects extreme points and critical points.
    🔗
    The direction of the implication in this theorem is important.

    Aside
    A statement and its converse
    🔗
    Here is the theorem, written in a very abbreviated form:
    \begin{equation*} \emph{statement}: \qquad \mbox{extreme} \Longrightarrow \mbox{critical}. \end{equation*}
    When we reverse the direction of the implication, we get a new statement, abbreviated the same way:
    \begin{equation*} \emph{converse}: \qquad \quad \mbox{critical} \Longrightarrow \mbox{extreme}. \end{equation*}
    The converse says that a critical point must be an extreme point.

    Aside
    The converse of this theorem is not true
    🔗
    But that is just not true. For example, an ordinary saddle point (a minimax) is a critical point, but it is not a minimum or a maximum.
    🔗
    The theorem and the observation

    Aside
    Searching critical points for extremes
    🔗
    about its converse are both important in the optimization process—that is, the search for extremes. Together they offer us the following guidance:
    🔗
    \(\bullet\) Search for the extremes of a function among its critical points.
    🔗
    \(\bullet\) A critical point may be neither a maximum nor a minimum.
    🔗
    To see how we can find extremes by searching among the critical points of a function, we’ll do a few examples. All the examples use the same basic idea. However, as the details get more complicated we bring in more powerful techniques.
    🔗

    [[EX:1]]..

    We’ll start with the function
    \begin{equation*} z = f(x, y) = x^3 - 4 x - y^2 \end{equation*}
    we have frequently used as a test case in this chapter.
    🔗
    The critical points of \(f\) are the points that simultaneously satisfy the two equations
    \begin{align*} dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} \amp= 3 x^2 - 4 = 0,\\ \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} \amp= - 2 y = 0. \end{align*}
    🔗
    It is clear that \(y = 0\) and \(x = \pm \sqrt{4/3} \approx \pm 1.1547\text{.}\) The two critical points are therefore
    \begin{equation*} \left(+\sqrt{4/3}, 0\right) \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad \left(-\sqrt{4/3}, 0\right). \end{equation*}
    If you check the contour plot you can see that \((-\sqrt{4/3}, 0)\) is a local maximum and \((+\sqrt{4/3}, 0)\) is a saddle point.
    🔗
    🔗

    [[EX:2]]..

    Here is a somewhat more complicated function:
    \begin{equation*} g(x, y) = 2 x^2 y - y^2 - 4 x^2 + 3 y. \end{equation*}
    The critical points are the solutions of the equations
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x} = 4 x y - 8 x = 0, \qquad \qquad \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y} = 2 x^2 - 2 y + 3 = 0. \end{equation*}
    Algebraic methods will still work, even though both variables appear in both equations. For example, we can rewrite \({\partial g/\partial y} = 0\) as
    \begin{equation*} 2y = 2 x^2 + 3 \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad y = x^2 + \tfrac{3}{2}. \end{equation*}
    We can then substitute this expression for \(y\) into \({\partial g/\partial x} = 0\) and get
    \begin{equation*} 4x \left(x^2 + \tfrac{3}{2}\right) - 8x = 4x \left( x^2 + \tfrac{3}{2} - 2 \right) = 4x \left( x^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} \right) = 0. \end{equation*}
    This implies \(x = 0\) or \(x = \pm \sqrt{1/2}\text{.}\) For each \(x\) we can then find the corresponding \(y\) by from the equation \(y = x^2 + \tfrac{3}{2}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Let’s work through this a second time using a geometric approach. The equations \({\partial g/\partial x} = 0\) and \({\partial g/\partial y} = 0\) both define curves in the \(x, y\)-plane. The curve \({\partial g/\partial y} = 0\) is a parabola: \(y = x^2 + \tfrac{3}{2}\text{.}\) The equation \({\partial g/\partial x} = 0\) factors as
    \begin{equation*} 4 x ( y - 2) = 0 \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad x ( y - 2 ) = 0. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now a product equals 0 precisely when one of its factors equals 0, so \({\partial g/\partial x} = 0\) implies that either \(x = 0\) or \(y - 2 = 0\text{.}\) In other words, the “curve” \({\partial g/\partial x} = 0\) consists of two lines:
    \begin{align*} x \amp= 0, \amp\quad \amp\mbox{a vertical line},\\ y \amp= 2, \amp\quad \amp\mbox{a horizontal line}. \end{align*}
    The two curves are shown in the figure at the right. They intersect in three points. (The place where the horizontal and vertical lines cross is not one of the intersection points.)
    🔗
    \begin{equation*} \left(-\sqrt{1/2}, 2 \right), \quad \left(\vphantom{\sqrt{1/2}} 0, \tfrac{3}{2} \right), \quad \left(+\sqrt{1/2}, 2 \right). \end{equation*}
    A glance at the contour plot of \(g\) makes it clear that the first and third of these are saddle points. The middle point is a local maximum. There are several ways to determine this. One is to look at the graph of \(g\text{.}\) Another is to look at a vertical slice of the graph through the line \(x = 0\text{.}\) Then \(z = g(0, y) = -y^2 + 3y\text{.}\) Here \(z\) has a maximum when \(y = \tfrac{3}{2}\text{.}\)

Now, however, we can specify its location exactly, because we have analytical tools for finding critical points. As the next example shows, these tools are useful even when we can’t carry out the algebraic manipulations.

    The equations for the critical points are
    🔗

    Subsection 9.3.4 The Method of Steepest Ascent

    Here is yet another way to find the maximum

    Aside
    Walk uphill to get to a maximum
    🔗
    of a function \(z = f(x, y)\text{.}\) Imagine that the graph of \(f\) is a landscape that you’re standing on. You want to get to the highest point. To do that you just walk uphill. But the uphill direction on the landscape is given by the gradient vector field of \(f\) (see page ). So you move to higher ground by following the gradient field.
    🔗
    In fact, the gradient field defines a

    Aside
    Trajectories of the gradient dynamical system…
    🔗
    🔗
    dynamical system of exactly the sort we studied in chapter 8. The differential equations are
    \begin{align*} dfrac{dx}{dt} \amp= \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(x, y),\\ [3pt] \dfrac{dy}{dt} \amp= \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(x, y). \end{align*}
    Because the gradient points uphill,

    Aside
    …lead to the local maxima
    🔗
    the trajectories of this dynamical system also go uphill. Trajectories flow to the attractors of the system; these are the local maxima of the function. Furthermore, since the gradient points in the direction \(f\) increases most rapidly, the trajectories follow paths of steepest ascent to the maxima. This explains the name of the method.
    🔗

    [[EX:1]]..

    Let’s see how the method of steepest ascent will find the local maxima of the function
    \begin{equation*} z = h(x, y) = x^2 y^2 - x^4 - y^4 - 2x^2 + 5 xy + y \end{equation*}
    🔗
    we considered in the previous example. The gradient field is
    \begin{align*} dfrac{dx}{dt} \amp= 2 x y^2 - 4 x^3 - 4 x + 5 y.\\ [3pt] \dfrac{dy}{dt} \amp= 2 x^2 y - 4 y^3 + 5 x + 1. \end{align*}
    As you can see at the left, some of the trajectories flow to a local maximum near \((-1, -1)\text{,}\) while other flow to the maximum whose position we determine on the opposite page. Each attractor has its own basin of attraction (as described in chapter 8). Therefore, the maximum found by the method of steepest ascent depends on the initial point of the trajectory.
    🔗
    If we replace the gradient vectors by their negatives,

    Aside
    The method of steepest descent
    🔗
    the new field will point directly downhill—to the local minima. Using the trajectories of the negative gradient field to find the minima is thus called the method of steepest descent. In the following example we use this method to investigate an economic question.
    🔗
    🔗

    [[EX:2]]..

    Manufacturing companies ship their products to regional warehouses from large distribution centers. Suppose a company has regional warehouses at \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\text{,}\) as shown on the map at the right. Where should it put its distribution center \(X\) so as to minimize the total cost of supplying the three regional warehouses?
    🔗
    This is a complicated problem that depends on many factors. For example, \(X\) probably should be put near major roads. The managers may also want to choose a location where labor costs are lower. Certainly, the total distance between the center and the three warehouses is important. Let’s simply get a first approximation to a solution by concentrating on the last factor.

    Aside
    Minimize the total distance
    🔗
    We will find the position for \(X\) that minimizes the total straight-line distance (the distance “as the crow flies”) from \(X\) to the three points \(A\text{,}\) \(B\text{,}\) and \(C\text{.}\) The map shows these distances as three dotted lines.
    🔗
    To describe the various positions we have introduced a coordinate system in which
    \begin{equation*} A: \ (0, 0), \qquad B: \ (6, 9), \qquad C: \ (10, 2). \end{equation*}
    The coordinates here are arbitrary. That is, they don’t represent miles, or kilometers, or any of the usual units of distance—but they are proportional to the usual units, so we can measure with them. If we let the unknown position of \(X\) be \((x, y)\text{,}\) then we seek to minimize the function
    \begin{equation*} S(x, y) = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2} + \sqrt{(x-6)^2 + (y-9)^2} + \sqrt{(x-10)^2 + (y-2)^2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    According to the method of steepest descent, we want to find the attractor of this dynamical system:
    \begin{align*} dfrac{dx}{dt} \amp= - \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2\vphantom{(y)^2)}}} - \dfrac{x - 6}{\sqrt{(x-6)^2 + (y-9)^2}} - \dfrac{x - 10}{\sqrt{(x-10)^2 + (y-2)^2}},\\ \dfrac{dy}{dt} \amp= - \dfrac{y}{\sqrt{x^2 + y^2\vphantom{(y)^2)}}} - \dfrac{y - 9}{\sqrt{(x-6)^2 + (y-9)^2}} - \dfrac{y - 2}{\sqrt{(x-10)^2 + (y-2)^2}}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    As you can see from the vector field of the dynamical system

    Aside
    The attractor is a global minimum
    🔗
    (above left), there is a single attractor, near the point \((6, 4)\text{.}\) This implies the total distance function \(S(x, y)\) has a single global minimum—which is what our intuition about the problem would lead us to expect.
    🔗
    To find the position of \(X\) more exactly, you can do the following. First, obtain a solution \((x(t), y(t))\) to the system of differential equations with an arbitrary initial condition—for example,
    \begin{equation*} x(0) = 1, \qquad y(0) = 1. \end{equation*}
    Then, obtain the coordinates of the

    Aside
    The attractor is the limit point of a solution
    🔗
    attractor by evaluating \((x(t), y(t))\) for larger and larger values of \(t\text{,}\) stopping when the values of \(x(t)\) and \(y(t)\) stabilize. You will find that
    \begin{equation*} X = \lim_{t \to \infty}(x(t), y(t)) = (6.22120\ldots, 3.96577\ldots). \end{equation*}
    🔗
    The important point to note here is that it is not necessary to plot the vector field—or any other graphic aid, like a contour plot or graph.

    Aside
    The method needs only a differential equation solver
    🔗
    You simply need to solve a system of differential equations. For example, the values above were found by modifying the computer program SIRVALUE we introduced in chapter 2. In summary: the method of steepest descent (or ascent) requires no graphical tools, but only a basic differential equation solver.

    Subsection 9.3.5 Lagrange Multipliers

    In searching for the interior extremes of a function \(f(x, y)\text{,}\) we have seen that it is helpful to solve the critical point equations:
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x} = 0, \qquad \quad \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y} = 0. \end{equation*}
    There is a similar set of equations

    Aside
    Equations for a constrained extreme
    🔗
    we can use in the search for the constrained extremes of \(f\text{.}\) These equations involve a new variable called a Lagrange multiplier.
    🔗
    So, suppose the function \(f(x, y)\) has an extreme on the constraint curve \(g(x, y) = 0\) at the point \((a, b)\text{.}\) According to the following diagram, the gradient vectors \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) must be parallel at \((a, b)\text{.}\)

    Aside
    \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) must be parallel at a point where \(f\) has an extreme along the constraint curve \(g = 0\)
    🔗
    (This means they are in the same direction or in opposite directions.) Here is why. We already know (see page ) that the level curve of \(f\) that passes through the constrained maximum or minimum must be tangent to the constraint curve. Now, the gradient vector \(\nabla f\) at any point is perpendicular to the level curve of \(f\) through that point—and the same is true for \(g\text{.}\) At a point where the level curves are tangent, the gradients \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) are perpendicular to the same curve, and must therefore be parallel.
    🔗
    Parallel vectors are multiples of each other.

    Aside
    The multiplier equation
    🔗
    Specifically, at a point \((a, b)\) where \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\) are parallel, there must be a number \(\lambda\) for which
    \begin{equation*} \nabla g (a, b) = \lambda \cdot \nabla f (a, b). \end{equation*}
    The multiplier \(\lambda\) is called a Lagrange multiplier. In the figure above, \(\lambda \approx 1/2\text{.}\) If \(\nabla g\) and \(\nabla f\) were in opposite directions, then \(\lambda\) would be negative.
    🔗

    Aside
    Joseph Louis Lagrange (1736–1813) was a French mathematician and a younger contemporary of Leonhard Euler. Like Euler he played an important role in making calculus the primary analytical tool for study the physical world. He is particularly noted for his contributions to celestial mechanics, the field where Isaac Newton first applied the calculus.
    🔗
    If we write out the multiplier equation using the components of \(\nabla f\) and \(\nabla g\text{,}\) we get
    \begin{equation*} \left(\dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b), \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) \right) = \lambda \left(\dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b), \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) \right) = \left(\lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b), \lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) \right). \end{equation*}
    In a vector equation, the vectors are equal component by component. Thus,
    \begin{align*} dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b) \amp= \lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b)\\ [4pt] \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) \amp= \lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b) \end{align*}
    🔗
    Let’s return to the main question,

    Aside
    How can we find a constrained maximum or minimum?
    🔗
    which can be stated this way: How do we determine where the function \(f(x, y)\) has a maximum or a minimum, subject to the constraint \(g(x, y) = 0\text{?}\) If we let \((a, b)\) denote the point we seek, then we see that \(a\) and \(b\) satisfy three equations:
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{rl} \mbox{the constraint equation}: & \hspace{24pt} g(a, b) = 0, \\[7pt] \mbox{the multiplier equations}: & \left\{ \begin{array}{l} \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial x}(a, b) = \lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial x}(a, b), \\[14pt] \dfrac{\partial g}{\partial y}(a, b) = \lambda \, \dfrac{\partial f}{\partial y}(a, b). \end{array} \right. \end{array} \end{equation*}
    In fact, there are three unknowns in these equations: \(a\text{,}\) \(b\text{,}\) and \(\lambda\text{.}\) When we solve the three equations for the three unknowns, we will determine the location of the constrained extreme. (We’ll also have a piece of information we can throw away: the value of \(\lambda\text{.}\))
    🔗

    Example..

    Find the maximum of \(f(x, y) = x^p y^{1-p}\) subject to the constraint \(x + y = c\text{.}\) There are two parameters in this problem: \(p\) and \(c\text{.}\) We assume that \(0 < p < 1\) and \(0 < c\text{.}\) We introduce parameters to remind you that analytic methods (such as Lagrange multipliers) are especially valuable in solving problems that depend on parameters.
    🔗
    We let \(g(x, y) = x + y - c\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \nabla g = (1, 1), \qquad \quad \nabla f = \left(p x^{p-1} y^{1-p}, (1-p) x^p y^{-p} \right), \end{equation*}
    so the three equations we must solve are
    \begin{align*} x + y - c \amp= 0,\\ 1 \amp= \lambda p x^{p-1} y^{1-p},\\ 1 \amp= \lambda (1-p) x^p y^{-p}. \end{align*}
    Since the second and third equations both equal 1, we can set them equal to each other:
    \begin{equation*} \lambda p x^{p-1} y^{1-p} = \lambda (1-p) x^p y^{-p}. \end{equation*}
    We can cancel the two \(\lambda\)s and combine the powers of \(x\) and \(y\) to get
    \begin{equation*} p x^{-1} y = 1 - p \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad \dfrac{y}{x} = \dfrac{1 - p}{p}. \end{equation*}
    According to the first of the three equations, \(y = c - x\text{.}\) If we substitution this expression in for \(y\) into the last equation, we get
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{c - x}{x} = \dfrac{1 - p}{p} \qquad \mbox{or} \qquad p(c - x) = (1 - p) x. \end{equation*}
    This equation reduces to \(pc = x\text{,}\) which gives us the \(x\)-coordinate of the maximum. To get the \(y\)-coordinate, we use \(y = c - x = c - pc = c(1 - p)\text{.}\) To sum up, the maximum is at
    \begin{equation*} (x, y) = (cp, c(1-p)) = c(p, 1-p). \end{equation*}

    The best-fitting line.

    Suppose we’ve taken measurements of two quantities \(x\) and \(y\text{,}\) and obtained the results shown in the table and graph below. We assume that \(y\) depends on \(x\) according to some rule that we don’t happen to know. In particular, we’d like to know what \(y\) is when \(x = 5\text{.}\) We have no data. Can we predict what \(y\) should be?
    🔗
     Here is a common approach to the question. We assume there is a simple underlying relation between \(y\) and \(x\text{.}\) However, the measurements that give us the data contain errors or “noise” of some sort that obscure the relationship. The simplest relation is a linear function, so we assume that there is a formula \(Y = mx + b\) that describes the connection between \(x\) and \(y\text{.}\)
    🔗
    Which line should we choose? In other words, how should we choose \(m\) and \(b\text{?}\) Since the data points don’t lie on a line, there is no perfect solution. For any choices, we must expect a difference \(e_j\) between the the \(j\)-th data value \(y_j\) and the value \(Y_j = m \, j + b\) predicted by the formula. These differences are the errors we assume are present.
    🔗
    A reasonable way to proceed is to minimize the total error. Even this involves choices. In the figure above, \(e_0\) and \(e_3\) are negative, so the ordinary total could be zero, or nearly so, even if the individual errors were large. We need a total that ignores the signs of the errors. Here is one:
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{absolute error:} \qquad |e_0| + |e_1| + |e_2| + |e_3| + |e_4| = AE. \end{equation*}
    Here is another:
    \begin{equation*} \mbox{squared error:} \qquad e_0^2 + e_1^2 + e_2^2 + e_3^2 + e_4^2 = SE. \end{equation*}
    In the following table we compare the data \(y_j\) with the calculated values \(Y_j\) and the resulting errors \(e_j\text{.}\)
    \begin{equation*} \begin{array}{cc|cc} x & y & Y & e = y - Y \\ [2pt] \hline 0 & 1 & \rule{0pt}{13pt}b & 1-b \\ 1 & 3 & \hphantom{1}m+b & 3-\hphantom{1}m-b \\ 2 & 4 & 2m+b & 4-2m-b \\ 3 & 3 & 3m+b & 3-3m-b \\ 4 & 5 & 4m+b & 5-4m-b \end{array} \end{equation*}
    To get the values of \(AE\) and \(SE\text{,}\)
