    Section 12.1 Stirling’s Formula

    Given a positive integer \(n\text{,}\) we define \(n!\)—pronounced \(n\) factorial—by the rule \(n!=1\cdot 2\cdot 3\cdots (n-1)\cdot n\) . This is a convenient concept which occurs in a number of settings,

    Aside
    Factorials in probability
    🔗
    particularly combinatorial and probabilistic ones. For instance, the probability of getting exactly \(n\) heads out of \(2n\) tosses of a coin turns out to be
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{(2n)!}{2^{2n}(n!)^2}. \end{equation*}
    Unfortunately, evaluating \(n!\) for values of \(n\) at all large is cumbersome at best. Although though many calculators will compute factorials, few of them can handle numbers as large as \(1000!\) . Even when we can evaluate \(n!\text{,}\) we are often as interested in the asymptotic behavior of a certain expression as much as in its exact value for specific \(n\text{.}\)

    Aside
    \(n!\) is difficult to calculate
    🔗
    For instance, using methods we develop below, it turns out that the above expression for the probability of \(n\) heads in \(2n\) tosses is very close to \(1/\sqrt{\pi n}\text{,}\) with the approximation being more accurate the larger \(n\) is. In fact, for \(n \geq 8\text{,}\) the approximation is good to two places; for \(n \geq 25\text{,}\) the approximation gives three-place accuracy.
    🔗
    In his book Methodus differentialis (1730), the British mathematician James Stirling published the following approximation, now know as Stirling’s formula, for the factorial operator:
    \begin{equation*} n!\sim \sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{-n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    While the right-hand side may look much more complicated than the left, think which one you would rather evaluate for, say, \(n=100\text{.}\) To see how good this approximation is, here are some comparisons:
    🔗
    As an example of the way elementary ideas in calculus can be used to derive powerful and subtle results, we will outline a derivation of Stirling’s approximation for \(n!\text{.}\) You should write up your own summary of this proof, filling in the gaps in the text below. We will work in two stages. In the first stage, we will show that
    \begin{equation*} n!\sim c\,n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\,e^{-n}. \end{equation*}
    for some constant \(c\text{.}\) In the second stage we will show that this constant is actually \(\sqrt{2\pi}\text{.}\)
    🔗

    Subsection 12.1.1 Stage One: Deriving the General Form

    We first observe that
    \begin{equation*} \ln (n!) = \ln 1 + \ln 2 + \ldots +\ln n. \end{equation*}
    It turns out to be easier to prove things about this logarithmic form. In fact, we will deal most easily with
    \begin{equation*} A_n=\ln 1 + \ln 2 + \ldots +\ln (n-1) + \dfrac{1}{2}\,\ln n. \end{equation*}
    Thus \(\ln(n!) = A_n+\tfrac{1}{2}\ln n \,.\) Even though \(\ln 1 = 0\text{,}\) it will be useful to retain the term in the expression for \(A_n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    We will find upper and lower bounds for \(A_n\) (and hence for \(\ln(n!)\) ) by approximating the area under the curve \(y=\ln x\) by certain inscribed and circumscribed trapezoids. We will then use these bounds to predict the asymptotic behavior of \(A_n\) for large values of \(n\text{.}\)
    🔗
    The upper bound: Note that if we inscribe a trapezoid under the graph of \(y=\ln x\) between \(x=k-1\) and \(x=k\text{,}\) its area will be \(\tfrac{1}{2}(\ln (k-1) +\ln k)\text{.}\) (How do we know that the straight line connecting the points \((k-1, \ln (k-1))\) and \((k, \ln k)\) will lie under the graph of \(y=\ln x\text{?}\)) The sum of the areas of all such trapezoids from \(x=1\) to \(x=n\) is clearly less than the area under the curve \(y=\ln x\) over the interval \([1,n]\text{.}\)   
    🔗
    The lower bound: On the other hand if we draw the tangent line to \(y=\ln x\) at \(x=k\) and form the trapezoid between \(x=k-.5\) and \(x=k+.5\text{,}\) its area will just be \(\ln k\) and will be greater than the area under the curve over the same interval. (We’ve used the fact—which you should check—that the area of a trapezoid equals the distance between the parallel sides times the distance between the midpoints of the other two sides.)
    🔗
     Adding up all such trapezoids, we get the inequality
    \begin{equation*} \int_{\frac{3}{2}}^n\ln x\,dx <A_n. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Since we know that \(\int \ln x\,dx = x\ln x -x\text{,}\) we can evaluate these upper and lower bounds to conclude
    \begin{equation*} n\ln n-n-\dfrac{3}{2}\ln \dfrac{3}{2} + \dfrac{3}{2} <A_n<n\ln n -n +1, \end{equation*}
    which in turn yields
    \begin{equation*} \left(n+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n-n+\dfrac{3}{2}\left(1-\ln \dfrac{3}{2}\right) <\ln n!<\left(n+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n -n +1. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Pause for a moment to observe that the difference
    \begin{equation*} D_n = \left(n+ \dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n -n +1-\ln n! \end{equation*}
    between the expressions on the two sides of the rightmost inequality is just the accumulated error from approximating the area under \(y=\ln x\) by the inscribed trapezoids. Since the error over each interval is always positive, \(D_n\) must therefore get larger as \(n\) increases, We will need this fact shortly.
    🔗
    Returning to our inequalities, they can finally be rewritten as
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{3}{2}\left(1-\ln \dfrac{3}{2}\right) < \ln n!-\left(n+ \dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n +n <1. \end{equation*}
    Evaluating the constants, we thus have that for any value of \(n\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} .8918<\ln n!-\left(n+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n +n <1. \end{equation*}
    If we exponentiate, this becomes
    \begin{equation*} 2.395<\dfrac{n!}{n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-n}} <2.719, \end{equation*}
    or
    \begin{equation*} 2.395 \, n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-n} < n! < 2.719 \, n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-n}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Notice that these bounds are already quite strong

    Aside
    These estimates are often adequate
    🔗
    and would be adequate for many estimates. Moreover, they are true for any value of \(n\text{.}\) If we are only interested in large values of \(n\text{,}\) we can do a little better. Let
    \begin{equation*} \delta_n=\ln n!-\left(n+\dfrac{1}{2}\right)\ln n +n. \end{equation*}
    Then \(1-\delta_n=(n+\frac{1}{2})\ln n -n +1-\ln n!\) is just the expression we called \(D_n\) a moment ago and said had to be increasing as \(n\) increases. But if \(D_n=1-\delta_n\) is increasing, it must be true that \(\delta_n\) itself is decreasing as \(n\) gets larger. We thus must have \(1>\delta_1>\delta_2>\ldots >\delta_n \ldots >.8918\text{.}\) There must therefore be some constant \(d\geq .8918\) such that \(\lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\delta_n=d\text{.}\) Define the constant \(c\) by \(c=e^d\text{.}\) Then
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n\rightarrow \infty}\dfrac{n!}{n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}e^{-n}} = c, \end{equation*}
    which is what we mean when we write
    \begin{equation*} n!\sim c\,n^{n+ \frac{1}{2}}\,e^{-n}. \end{equation*}
    This completes stage 1. In stage 2 we will see that \(c=\sqrt{2\pi}\text{.}\)
    🔗
    🔗

    Subsection 12.1.2 Stage Two: Evaluating \(c\)

    We will do this using an interesting result of a 17th century English mathematician, John Wallis,

    Aside
    Wallis’s formula
    🔗
    who showed that
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{2}{1} \times \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{4}{3}\times \dfrac{4}{5}\times \dfrac{6}{5} \times \dfrac{6}{7}\times \cdots \times \dfrac{2n}{2n-1} \times \dfrac{2n}{2n+1} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Suppose for the moment that we had proved Wallis’s formula. We can express it in terms of factorials by noting that we can rewrite the product of the first \(n\) even numbers—\(2\times 4\times 6\times \ldots \times (2n)\)—by factoring a \(2\) out of each term, leaving us
    \begin{equation*} 2\times 4\times 6\times \ldots \times (2n) = 2^n\,(n!). \end{equation*}
    Similarly, we can take the product of the first \(n\) odd integers—\(1\times 3\times 5\times 7\times \ldots \times (2n-1)\)—and insert the missing even terms to get
    \begin{align*} 1\times 3\times 5\times 7\times \ldots \times (2n-1) \amp= \dfrac{1\times 2\times 3\times 4\times \ldots \times (2n-1) \times (2n)} {2\times 4\times 6\times \ldots \times (2n)}\\ \amp= \dfrac{(2n)!}{2^n\,(n!)}. \end{align*}
    We can thus rewrite Wallis’s formula as
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{\left(2^n\,n!\right)^4}{\left((2n)!\right)^2(2n+1)}= \dfrac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    If we now replace all the factorials by their corresponding expressions using Stirling’s approximation, we get
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{2^{4n}c^4n^{4n+2}e^{-4n}}{c^2(2n)^{4n+1}e^{-4n}(2n+1)}= \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    which, after a great deal of cancelation, reduces to
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{c^2n}{2(2n+1)}= \dfrac{\pi}{2}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    Now since
    \begin{equation*} \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{n}{2n+1} = \dfrac{1}{2}, \end{equation*}
    this reduces to
    \begin{equation*} \dfrac{c^2}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \end{equation*}
    so
    \begin{equation*} c^2 = 2\pi, \end{equation*}
    and
    \begin{equation*} c=\sqrt{2\pi}, \end{equation*}
    as desired.
    🔗

    Deriving Wallis’s formula.

    One way to derive Wallis’s formula involves the integrals
    \begin{equation*} I_k = \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^kx\,dx. \end{equation*}
    Note that \(I_0>I_1>I_2>I_3>\ldots .\) Moreover, you should verify that
    \begin{equation*} I_0 = \dfrac{\pi}{2} \qquad \mbox{and} \qquad I_1=1. \end{equation*}
    Using the reduction formula derived in chapter 11.5 for antiderivatives of \(\sin^n{x}\text{,}\) we have a similar reduction formula for the \(I_k\text{:}\)
    \begin{align*} I_k \amp= \int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^kx\,dx\\ \amp= \left. \dfrac{-1}{k}\sin^{k-1}x\,\cos x \, \right|_0^{\pi/2} + \dfrac{k-1}{k} \int_0^{\pi/2} \sin^{k-2}x\,dx\\ \amp= \dfrac{k-1}{k} I_{k-2}. \end{align*}
    🔗
    This in turn leads to
    \begin{equation*} I_k = \begin{cases} \dfrac{2n-1}{2n}\cdot \dfrac{2n-3}{2n-2}\cdots \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot \dfrac{\pi}{2} & \mbox{if $k=2n$ is even}, \\[.15in] \dfrac{2n}{2n+1}\cdot \dfrac{2n-2}{2n-1}\cdots \dfrac{2}{3}\cdot 1 & \mbox {if $k=2n+1$ is odd}. \end{cases} \end{equation*}
    Further, note that
    \begin{equation*} I_{2n+2}/I_{2n}= \dfrac{2n+1}{2n+2}, \end{equation*}
    which has the limit 1 for large \(n\text{.}\) Since \(I_{2n}>I_{2n+1}>I_{2n+2}\text{,}\) it follows that \(I_{2n+1}/I_{2n}\) approaches 1 for large \(n\text{.}\) But this gives us
    \begin{align*} 1 \amp= {\displaystyle\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}I_{2n+1}/I_{2n}}\\ [4pt] \amp= {\displaystyle \lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \left(\dfrac{2n}{2n+1}\cdot \dfrac{2n-2}{2n-1}\cdots \dfrac{2}{3}\cdot 1\right) \div \left(\dfrac{2n-1}{2n}\cdot \dfrac{2n-3}{2n-2}\cdots \dfrac{1}{2}\cdot \dfrac{\pi}{2}\right)}\\ [4pt] \amp= {\displaystyle\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty} \dfrac{2n}{2n+1}\cdot \dfrac{2n}{2n-1}\cdot \dfrac{2n-2}{2n-1}\cdots \dfrac{2n-2}{2n-3} \cdots \dfrac{2}{3}\cdot \dfrac{2}{1}\cdot \dfrac{2}{\pi}} \end{align*}
    If we multiply both sides of this equation by \(\pi/2\text{,}\) we get Wallis’s formula.
    🔗
    🔗

    \begin{equation*} n!\sim \sqrt{2\pi}\,n^{n+\frac{1}{2}}\, e^{-n+\frac{1}{12n}-\frac{1}{360n^3}+\frac{1}{1260n^5}-\cdots}. \end{equation*}

    Subsection 12.1.3 The Binomial Distribution

    🔗
    One of the most frequently encountered concepts in probability theory is the binomial probability distribution. Suppose we repeat a certain experiment—flipping a penny, rolling a single die, mating a pair of fruit flies, feeding cholesterol to a lab rat—over and over. Suppose further that there is some outcome we are looking for—getting heads, rolling a 2, getting a red-eyed offspring, developing liver cancer in the rat—in each experiment. If \(p\) is the probability \(p\) of obtaining the looked-for outcome in any one experiment, denote by \(P(n, k, p)\) the probability of the outcome happening exactly \(k\) times in \(n\) experiments. It turns out that
    \begin{equation*} P(k,n, p)=\dfrac{n!}{k!(n-k)!}p^k(1-p)^{n-k}. \end{equation*}
    🔗
    [[EX:1]]How likely is it to get four 2’s if we roll twelve dice? The probability of getting a 2 by throwing one die is \(\dfrac{1}{6}\text{.}\) Therefore the answer to the question is
    \begin{equation*} P(12, 4, \tfrac{1}{6})= \dfrac{12!}{4!\, 8!} \left(\dfrac{1}{6}\right)^4\left(\dfrac{5}{6}\right)^8=.0888281 \end{equation*}
    —we should get exactly four 2’s slightly less frequently than once out of every 11 times we roll twelve dice.
    🔗
    [[EX:2]]What is the probability of getting exactly 47 heads if we flip 100 pennies? Since the probability of getting heads on a single toss of a penny is \(\frac{1}{2}\text{,}\)
    \begin{equation*} P(100, 47, \tfrac{1}{2}) = \dfrac{100!}{47!\,53!}\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)^{100}=.0665905, \end{equation*}
    —on the average, if we flip 100 pennies, we should get 47 heads about once out of every 15 times.
    🔗
    The second example demonstrates the fact that calculating binomial probabilities can get very messy very quickly. Several of the exercises are designed to show how Stirling’s formula can give us quick estimates that are easy to calculate and work with.
